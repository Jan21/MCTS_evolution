Two planners, one board

The puzzle. Four robots sit on a walled grid. A robot slides in a straight line when you push it and only stops at a wall, at the board edge, or against another robot. One robot is the target; it has to reach the marked cell. The score is the number of slides used, so fewer is better.

The two planners. Both search with the same trained networks and the same budget. The one on the left plans only in sub-goals — statements of the form “get robot A to stop at cell C, using robot B as the thing that stops it”. That language is expressive but it cannot say “just push the red robot right, and see what that gives you”. The one on the right, the hybrid, is allowed to make one or two ordinary robot slides first, and only then plans in sub-goals from the position that results.

What to watch for. On the highlighted puzzles the hybrid's very first move is an ordinary slide of a robot that is often not even the target robot. It costs one move. It then makes the rest of the problem much easier, and the total comes out shorter — sometimes shorter than the best plan the sub-goal language can express at all. Step through both sides at once and compare where the robots end up after the same number of moves.

Using the page. Choose a group, then a puzzle. The two boards step together, so after the same number of moves you can see how far each planner has got. Arrow keys drive the playback; a puzzle can belong to more than one group.
