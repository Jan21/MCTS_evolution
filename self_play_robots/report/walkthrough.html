Part 1 — the ground

What one puzzle is

Ricochet Robots is a single-player puzzle on a square grid of n by n cells. Walls sit on cell edges. The four outer edges are always walls. Several robots stand on distinct cells. One robot is the target robot. One empty cell is the goal cell.

A move (also called a slide) is a pair of one robot and one direction. The robot slides until a wall, the board edge, or another robot stops it. The robot cannot stop where you want. The puzzle is solved when the target robot stands on the goal cell. Fewer moves is better.

The consequence of the sliding rule is that robots serve as bumpers. To stop the target robot on the goal cell, another robot often has to stand on the cell just past the goal first. Solutions therefore contain helper moves that have nothing to do with the target robot's own path.

Two objects must stay separate. A board is the geometry alone: the grid size and the wall layout. An instance (also called a puzzle) is a board plus a start placement: all robot cells, which robot is the target, and the goal cell. Instances live one per line in JSON Lines files. Each line carries a field d_star, written d* in this page, which is the exact move optimum when it is known. No planner ever reads d_star at solve time.

Board-id splits, and lean boards (why sizes above 32x32 exist at all)

Two split conventions exist. g16r4 inherits the legacy ids of the original 16x16 pipeline: train 0-95 and 1000-1799, validation 1800-2399, test 112-127 and 2400-2999, benchmark 2400-2549. Every other configuration uses the standard convention over 1200 boards: train 0-699, validation 700-899, test and benchmark 900-1049, spare 1050-1199. Test and benchmark are the same 150 boards. The two conventions use disjoint id blocks, so they must never be described as one range. The self-play project adds a third rule: generation uses ids 40000-49999, examination uses ids 20000-20999, and nothing may ever train on 0-1199 or on 20000 and above.

Lean boards. A standard board pickle stores two all-pairs distance tables. Building them costs about O(n^4 log n). The measured cold build cost is 8.2 s at 16x16, 60 s at 24x24 and 222 s at 32x32, and a log-log fit projects about 1.7 h at 64x64 and about 11.9 h at 96x96. A full 1200-board 64x64 set would need about 230 GB of sidecar tables. A lean board drops the tables and answers a distance query on demand. Measured lean cost per board is 0.24 s at 32x32, 1.5 s at 64x64 and 3.8 s at 96x96, with pickles of 1.1 MB, 4.9 MB and 11.7 MB. A lazy query costs 1.43 times a dictionary lookup and returns bit-identical results, checked exhaustively over 1.45 M table entries at 16x16 and 24x24 with zero mismatches. Lean boards are layout-identical to standard boards by construction, because the two table builders consume no randomness. The configurations at 40x40 and above exist only as lean boards.

The benchmarks, and the graded / frontier split

Every pinned benchmark holds 450 instances drawn from 150 boards, 3 instances per board. At benchmark construction time an exact solver attempts each instance. Where it succeeds, the instance receives a d_star. Where it fails, the instance keeps a placeholder. The file is then split in two.

  • The graded set (bench.solved.jsonl) holds the instances with a known optimum. Every quality metric is computable there.
  • The frontier set (bench.unsolved.jsonl) holds the instances the exact solver could not grade. No optimum exists there, at any budget. Only solve rate, search cost and wall clock mean anything.

Realized strict moves is the definition that changed the project

A subgoal planner does not produce moves. It produces a plan, which is a graph of subgoals. A plan is complete when no part of it is left unresolved. A complete plan is still an abstraction, and it may not be playable.

strict_moves is the legal joint-game realization and the metric of record. It orders the plan's physical segments so that a helper reaches its support cell before the mover bounces off it, then executes one segment at a time on the full board with every other robot as a blocker. It returns the total slide count, or nothing at all if any segment is unreachable. A subgoal instance counts as solved only when strict_moves returns a number.

The measured consequence is the whole shape of the project. The subgoal planner's historical 99.6 % solve rate counted plans that could not be played. Forced through strict realization it fell to 53.1 % of 450. Four verified defect fixes later it reached 80 %, and a playability check inside the search reached 89.1 %.

Every arena result file is also replayed by an independent checker before the project accepts it. That checker imports only the physics layer, replays the dumped move list from the start positions, and requires that every move is a legal full slide, that the sequence length equals the reported move count, and that the target robot ends on the goal. A failure quarantines the file and stops the run.

The six ways a complete plan fails, and the two counters that are not the metric of record

A complete plan can fail realization in six ways: the segment's robot cannot reach the segment end with every other robot as a blocker, no reachable certified cell exists before a split segment's bounce, the leg after the support is placed is unreachable, the dependency graph has no topological order, everything executes but the target robot is not on the goal cell, or the plan has no physical segments at all.

abstract_moves is a diagnostic, not the metric of record. It costs each segment on its own with only its intended support robot on the board. It can under-count real moves, so a regret computed from it can be negative. A counter named n_negative_abstract_regret exists purely to catch that case.

Label regret is a second, unrelated quantity that shares the name. It is the cost-to-go gap between the candidate a value network ranks first and the truly best candidate in the same decision. It is measured in label cost units, not in game moves, and it is not comparable to move regret.

Expansions, and the granularity asymmetry the project states itself

Both planner families are best-first searches. They are compared at a fixed budget so that neither can simply buy a better result. The unit is the expansion, defined in every result file as one popped search node whose children are generated, which costs one policy pass plus one batched value pass over at most k children. k is 5 in every result cited on this page. The standard budget is 1200 expansions.

The project records the asymmetry in its own generated protocol note. One subgoal expansion commits an entire subgoal, which is worth several primitive moves. One move-by-move expansion commits a single primitive move. A shared expansion cap therefore grants the subgoal planner strictly more solution-building work per step. That is the conservative direction for the headline comparison. It is also the reason why wall-clock time is always reported next to expansions and never instead of it.

Two honest caveats about the expansion counter, and what the timer includes

Over-count. The counter increments before candidate generation. A pop that produces no candidates still costs one expansion while making zero network passes.

Derivation on the move-by-move side. Under eval.compare the move planner's expansions are not counted but derived from the number of network calls, as (calls - 1) // 2. The cap actually passed to the search increments even on a pop with no successors, so the reported expansion count can be lower than the iterations actually spent. The self-play bench runner keeps both counters per row and cross-checks them.

Free work. Three kinds of work are explicitly free and are recorded in separate accounting fields: forced exact fixes where a segment turns out to have a known exact path, the final pop of a complete plan, and all physics work such as strict realization, prefix checks and park repairs.

The timer. It starts after the board is loaded and the state is built, and stops when the search returns. It excludes checkpoint loading, board loading and state construction. It includes the whole search loop and all network passes. In anytime mode it also includes realization, park repairs and prefix checks, because those run inside the search.

The exact move oracle: score, timings, and where it stops

When this solver returns a number, that number is the exact minimum. Its heuristic is admissible, edges have unit cost, and the goal test happens on pop. When it cannot finish it returns nothing at all. It never returns an approximation. Its score is therefore the same as the graded share of each benchmark in the table above: it grades 450 of 450 at g16r4 by construction, and between 161 and 316 of 450 at the other five rungs.

Where more budget helps, measured once. All 184 oracle failures at g16r8 were re-run at ten times the budget. 75 of 184 (40.8 %) then solved, at mean d* 8.23 and maximum 10, which is harder than the graded set's mean of 5.19. 109 of 184 (59.2 %) stayed unsolved. No equivalent probe exists at any other rung.

Timing. No per-instance timing file for this solver survives on disk. Two indirect facts exist. The 60 second wall-time cap means one failure at g24r8 costs up to 60 s, and a repository analysis note states that benchmark construction cost is dominated by oracle failures at 60 s each. Separately, the exact move labelling run at 64x64 reported a yield of about 11 solvable instances per 120 attempts at a 40,000-expansion budget, which is about 9 %.

Caps, the accepted non-determinism, and the reported speed-ups

Caps written into every work item: 14 candidates per decision, 4,000 pops of the plan-space search, a 40,000-entry heap guard, a helper-dependent edge weight of 2, and a deterministic budget of 10,000,000 plan-solve iterations per rollout. The Rust engine refuses a wall-time cap outright, with the message that a timeout is a non-deterministic parity knob it does not implement. Determinism is preferred over a time limit.

The accepted limit. The inner plan search's cost estimate is not admissible, and the Python reference's candidate ordering depends on the iteration order of a Python set. The trajectory a rollout takes can therefore differ between two runs at equal-score tie points. The measured incidence is 12 records in 71,200, about 0.017 %. The project adjudicated and accepted this, because the reference itself is under-determined at those points.

Reported speed-ups against the Python reference: subgoal labelling 52.7x at g16r4, 178.9x at g16r6, 169.2x at g16r8, 108.0x at g24r4 and 79.9x at g32r4. Move labelling ran 58.4x to 79.9x faster after optimisation. The verification document adds two cautions: the g16r4 margin is load-sensitive and an independent re-run measured 50.4x, and an earlier "about 66x" move-labelling figure was unpaired and must not be quoted.

Board construction cost for the same runs, using lean boards, for 170 boards each: 20 s at 40x40, 31 s at 48x48, 45 s at 56x56, 63 s at 64x64.

Probe caps, timings, where it cannot run, and the older probe that disagrees

Caps: 200,000 pops per instance, a 400,000-entry heap guard, 60 s of wall clock per instance by default, 2 parks per repair, and 16 worker processes. The slack setting controls how far past the first certified plan the search keeps exploring. Re-running with slack of 4 and 12 moved the mean best-plan regret by at most 0.093 moves, so the bound is tight in practice.

Timing. The base vocabulary is nearly free. The whole 450-instance base benchmark finishes in 5.8 s on 16 workers, and the median instance takes no measurable time. The extended vocabulary is far more expensive on three of the four graded sets. Comparing total per-row seconds, it costs 65.9 times more at g16r4 (1113.4 s against 16.9 s), 45.1 times more at g24r4 (784.7 s against 17.4 s) and 57.0 times more at g32r4 (359.3 s against 6.3 s). At g24r8 the comparison inverts (88.5 s against 162.9 s), because three instances dominate the base arm: two on board 995 at 62.18 s and 58.17 s, and one on board 939 at 34.7 s, together 155.05 s of that arm's 162.92 s. On the frontier sets the extended-vocabulary median instance takes 11 to 22 s and a third to a half of all instances trip a cap.

Where it cannot run. The probe has no lean-board path. It cannot be pointed at the 40x40 to 96x96 configurations, and it cannot be pointed at the unseen exam's boards. There is therefore no ceiling number for the unseen exam.

The older probe. A second, earlier probe exists. It was applied only to the instances the planner failed. The two probes agree exactly on the base vocabulary at 16x16 with 4 robots: 408 of 450 realizable, with the identical 22 and 20 split of the two structural failure kinds, from two independent implementations. They differ on the extended vocabulary, 99.6 % against 98.0 %, which is a caps and scope difference and is listed in Part 4.

Cap sensitivity, checked. Every "no complete plan" verdict was re-probed at four times the plan-width cap. At 16x16 with 4 robots all 22 stayed structural. At 16x16 with 6 robots all 48 stayed structural, with one instance moving between the two structural categories. Zero of 70 became solvable. The ceiling is a property of the language and not of the search budget.

The hard limits of the ground truth

Two walls exist, and they bind at very different places.

Wall one, the size wall at 64. The exact engine packs the joint robot state into a fixed-width integer with 6 bits per coordinate, which addresses 0 to 63. assert!(n <= 64) is compiled into the release build. There is no configuration flag, no slower fallback and no approximation. Above 64 the study has no exact answer at any price.

Wall two, and it binds first. Well below 64, the exact solver already fails on most instances.

Part 2 — the two approaches, and the language

Two learned planner families exist. They differ in one thing only: what counts as an action. Everything else follows from that choice.

What each approach actually is

Move-by-move, called the forward planner

A search node is a board state, that is the joint position of every robot. An action is one robot slide. The action set has size 4 times the robot count, minus the slides that cannot leave their cell. The transition is the true multi-robot physics. Every move costs 1. The goal test is that the target robot stands on the goal cell.

One network with two heads drives the search. The value head predicts the exact optimal cost-to-go. The policy head predicts which robot and direction to play. The search is best-first with priority equal to moves so far plus the predicted cost-to-go. At each expansion the policy orders the legal successors and the search keeps the top 5.

The stopping rule is simple. The search returns as soon as it pops a goal state. Because the transition model is the real game, any returned path is playable by construction. There is no realization step and no realization failure.

Subgoals, called the backward planner

A search node is a partial plan, which is a directed acyclic graph. An action is one subgoal. A subgoal is a triple: a bottleneck cell that the moving robot must reach, a support cell that a helper robot must occupy, and the helper robot chosen to occupy it. Read as an English sentence, a subgoal says "park helper H on cell S, so that the moving robot stops on cell B".

Each edge of the plan carries a status. A fixed edge has an exact shortest path and an exact cost. An open edge has no exact path yet, carries an optimistic estimate, and still needs a subgoal. A plan is complete when it has no open edges. The search pops the cheapest plan, returns it if it is complete, and otherwise resolves its first open edge. It first tries to pin that edge to an exact path. If that fails, a proposal step generates candidate subgoals and each candidate spawns one child plan.

Two networks drive it. A proposal net ranks candidate subgoals. A value net predicts the cost to complete a partial plan. At each expansion the driver ranks all surviving candidates with one proposal pass, keeps the top 5, and scores those 5 with one batched value pass.

The stopping rule is not simple, and it is not the same in every result file. Three modes exist and they are not equivalent.

One worked subgoal example

Benchmark index 0, board 2400, at 16x16 with 4 robots. The exact optimum is d* = 12. Red starts at (1,15) and must reach (9,15). The plan holds two subgoals.

  1. Subgoal 1. Bottleneck (9,15) for Red, support (10,15) held by Blue. Red slides right and Blue stops it exactly on the goal cell. Red's leg costs 7 moves.
  2. Subgoal 2. Blue cannot reach (10,15) on its own. So the plan adds a nested subgoal: bottleneck (10,15) for Blue, support (11,15) held by Green. Blue's leg costs 2 moves. Green's leg costs 3 moves.

Total plan cost is 12. The strict realization plays 12 legal moves. Both equal d* = 12. This is what "the search commits a whole subgoal per expansion" means in practice. Two decisions produced a 12-move solution.

Fairness: which comparisons hold, and which do not

Four issues exist. Three favour the move-by-move planner and one favours the subgoal planner. All four are stated here in the visible text, because none of them is optional reading.

Issue 1. The stopping rules are not the same, and they are not even the same across subgoal arms. The exact search flags were extracted from the recorded command of every result file.

result filessearch variant actually used
base-vocabulary arms at g16r8, g24r4, g24r8, g32r4, the g16r6 frontier arm, and the base-configuration prefix file--backward-prefix-check
the g16r6 graded base arm, the base-configuration anytime file, every fixed-nets old-vocabulary file--backward-anytime

So the base-language arm and the extended-language arm differ in the stopping rule at four of six configurations. The move-by-move planner stops at its first goal state in every row. The direction of the effect is known: the anytime rule can only help solve rate, because it retries after a realization failure inside the same budget. At the base configuration the two variants give exactly the same count, 401 of 450, so the confound is empirically null there. At the other configurations it was never measured. This matters for the negative result at 24x24 below, where the anytime arm still lost.

Issue 2. The shared expansion cap gives the subgoal planner more work per step. One subgoal expansion commits a whole subgoal, which is worth several primitive moves. This is the conservative direction for the headline comparison and the project states it in its own generated protocol note.

Issue 3. Real work is performed that no expansion counter records, and all of it is on the subgoal side. Realization checks, prefix checks, park repairs, exact-fix pops and the per-board distance-table build all fall outside the counter. The size of this is measurable. At g24r4 the base prefix-check arm records 36.24 prefix-check calls per instance against 8.46 policy passes. The extended-language arm records 1.95 realization calls and 1.09 park-repair calls per instance against 14.29 policy passes.

The project measured the two stacks in a common unit, the one physics primitive both reach. On the same five base-configuration puzzles the subgoal arm performs a median of 64 physics slides and a mean of 3.4 expansions, and the move arm performs a median of 5,776 physics slides and a mean of 337.0 expansions. The subgoal search itself performs zero slide calls, because it plans over precomputed tables. The physics-only ratio is about 90x against an expansion ratio of about 99x, so the efficiency advantage survives a matched physics unit essentially unchanged. That said, this is a five-puzzle pilot and not a per-configuration measurement, and its move-planner arm is not the headline network.

Issue 4. Wall-clock time compares only inside one result file. Within one file both arms ran in the same job on the same machine. Across files they often did not, because the study moved to a new cluster on 2026-07-20. The size of the machine effect was measured directly: the g24r4 move-planner rows in the two files are identical in all 232 rows on solved status, move count and expansion count, and only the timings differ, at a median of 65.94 s against 87.62 s, a factor of 1.33.

The plan language, and the two effects that must stay apart

Two extensions were added to the subgoal vocabulary. The project calls them B1 and B2. Before any number, one distinction has to be fixed, because it is the single most important idea in this part.

  • Expressibility. How many instances the language can describe at all. An exhaustive search with no networks measures this. It answers the question: does a playable plan exist in this vocabulary?
  • Achievement. What a trained planner reaches at a fixed budget with that vocabulary available. A benchmark run measures this. It answers a different question: does the planner find such a plan inside 1200 steps?

The two extensions moved the first quantity a lot. They moved the second much less, and at one configuration they moved it the wrong way.

What the base language could not say, and what each extension adds

A helper robot must reach its support cell, and a robot can only stop at a wall or at another robot. The base vocabulary handles this by admitting only support cells that already have an adjacent wall. That is one line of code in the proposal step. Everything else in the stack was already general. So the base language cannot say: put the helper on a cell that only becomes holdable because a second robot goes there first.

Changes two and three pull in opposite directions. Anytime should help solve rate. Losing the prefix filter should hurt it. So this is not a clean single-variable measurement and it must not be presented as one. What is not in doubt is the direction at the level a reader cares about: the extended arm received the retry rule that can only help, received a strictly larger set of expressible plans, spent twice the search steps, and still solved six fewer puzzles and wrote solutions 1.59 moves longer where both arms solved.

Why the richer language did not reach the trained planner

Four measured mechanisms explain the gap. All four were verified against the source files.

  1. The by-reference machinery was never wired into the learned planner. The evaluation driver calls the proposal step without injecting the reference helpers and applies candidates without the by-reference flag. The wiring exists only in a branch the neural loop never reaches. The instrumented census settles it: across 46,351 expansions and 4,577,425 candidate applications, the shipped driver generated, ranked, shortlisted and expanded exactly zero by-reference candidates, while a correctly wired driver would have offered 2,451,324 of them, in 97.0 % of expansions and on 134 of 134 instances. Consequence: every "B2" row measured in the supervised study is really B1 plus generalized park repairs.
  2. The featurization cannot even name a by-reference candidate. The encoder maps a candidate's helper by its robot's start position, and the driver drops any candidate whose helper is not at a start cell. A by-reference helper never is, by definition.
  3. The policy net never trained on one. The trainer silently skips any record whose candidate helper is not at a start cell. Every by-reference record was filtered out of every corpus before the policy trainer saw it. Only the value net, which uses raw cell indices, saw them. The corpora do contain them, at a by-reference share of 12.7 % at g16r4, 11.0 % at g16r6, 10.4 % at g16r8, 10.5 % at g24r8 and 10.6 % at g32r4.
  4. Roughly half of the exact extended-vocabulary labels price plans that cannot be played. A review of the label pipeline sampled 16 depth-0 decisions covering 28 exact-optimal extended completions, and strict realization certified only 13 of 28, that is 46 %. The remaining 54 % are transient-support plans that the abstract language accepts and physics rejects. The exact labeller also never prices parks at all, because a park is a realization-time repair and not part of any labelling vocabulary.

The direct test of the first three mechanisms. The by-reference step was later wired into the driver and run as a matched A/B at g16r6 with the same banked networks and the same pinned instances. With the step off the arm solves 310 of 316 graded and 105 of 134 frontier. With it on the arm solves 310 of 316 graded and 108 of 134 frontier, while ranking 599.1 by-reference candidates per graded instance and 5363.3 per frontier instance. The pooled McNemar test gives p = 0.61. The supply is enormous and the effect is +3 frontier solves and 0 graded solves. The supply is not the bottleneck. The training signal is.

Retraining on extended-vocabulary labels was also tried at the base configuration. It moved the count by +3 solves and then by -4 solves of 450, and it increased the mean search effort from 9.7 to 14.6 and then 15.3 expansions. The project's own log records the same conclusion for the whole campaign: the zero-shot configuration remains the method's best at every rung measured.

One sentence for this section. A richer language raises what is possible. It does not by itself teach a planner to use it, and when its cost model predicts real moves worse, it can make a trained planner worse.

What Part 2 hands to Part 3. The base subgoal language is saturated, because the supervised planner already sits at or within a few solves of every exhaustive base ceiling. Both vocabularies have a measured quality floor far above the exact optimum, at about 1.26 and 1.62 moves for the base language and 0.76 and 1.10 for the extended language at 16x16 and 24x24, against a move-by-move planner regret of 0.067 and 0.068. So no subgoal-space planner, however well trained, can approach the move-by-move planner on move quality at these sizes. And the supervised route to the extended language is exhausted, because all four blocking mechanisms above are properties of the supervised pipeline. The remaining lever is a training signal generated by certified play rather than by an abstract cost model.

One round, as a numbered procedure

  1. Write fresh boards. The generator writes 60 new lean boards using the round number as the random seed. Round k uses board ids 8000 + 60(k-1) through 8000 + 60k - 1.
  2. Refuse any board id that could leak into an exam. Before anything else, the code collects every id at or below 1199 and raises a fatal error if that list is not empty. Every pinned exam board of every configuration lives in the range 0-1199, and the graded and frontier exams use ids 900-1049. This is a hard failure and not a warning.
  3. Start 6 worker processes. Each worker is a fresh process. Each loads its own copy of the policy net and the value net onto the GPU, builds the B2 solver, and enables by-reference helper resolution.
  4. Draw instances. For each board a worker samples robot cells and a goal cell uniformly. There is no difficulty filter and no curriculum. The worker keeps drawing until 8 instances have produced at least one training record, and it gives up after 24 attempts.
  5. Search one instance with a PUCT tree search at these settings: budget 300 expansions, width k = 5, keep the cheapest certified plan rather than the first, exploration constant 1.5, backup by minimum, Dirichlet root noise at weight 0.25 and alpha 0.3, all candidates considered at the root rather than the top 5, stop after 80 expansions with no improvement, park repairs enabled, and a per-instance wall-clock cap of 600 seconds.
  6. Certify every complete plan. When selection reaches a complete plan, the certifier calls strict realization, which executes the plan segment by segment on the full joint state with real slide physics. If it returns a number, the node becomes solved and that number propagates up the path. If it returns nothing, the node is dead, unless park repairs produce repaired variants, which then re-enter the search as ordinary complete plans.
  7. Extract records from the tree. Follow the principal path from the root, that is the chain of children whose best certified result matches the parent's. At each node on that path, examine every child that was a real decision. A child is labelled only if some complete plan below it was strictly realized. If a child was never certified, a greedy completion attempts to finish it in at most 16 steps and passes the result to the same certifier. A decision emits a training group only when at least 2 of its children were labelled.
  8. Write the round's data atomically, plus a manifest and a per-instance statistics file.
  9. Run the fidelity gauge. Sample up to 200 depth-0 decisions from the round, label the same decisions exactly with the Rust engine, and compare.
  10. Build the training buffer from the record files of the last 3 rounds. Inside each round the board ids are sorted and the last 15 % of the ids become validation. Validation is therefore board-disjoint from training by construction.
  11. Train both nets, once for the policy and once for the value net, both warm-started from the previous round's checkpoint: 6 epochs, learning rate 1e-4, AdamW at weight decay 1e-4 with no scheduler, batch of 8 decision groups for the policy and 4 groups for the value net, and checkpoint selection on minimum validation regret. The policy target is a softmax over the group's candidates. The value target is a 96-bin distribution plus a within-group ranking loss. A collapse guard halts the value net after 3 epochs with a validation group spread below 0.05.
  12. Bench and gate. Bench the new nets on the graded exam and on the frontier exam with two searches, best-first and PUCT, both at 1200 expansions and width 5. Merge the shards, then run the independent replay validator on the merged payload. If replay fails, quarantine the file and fail the job. Finally run a paired comparison against the previous round: an exact McNemar test on the solved vectors and a sign test on moves over the instances both rounds solved.

Where the training answers come from, and what makes a bad label impossible

The answer is measurement and not network prediction. The label of a labelled child is the abstract cost of the certified plan below it, minus the cost already fixed at that decision. That certified plan exists only because the physics engine executed it.

Three code facts exclude a wrong label, where "wrong" means a claim that a plan reaches the goal when it does not.

  1. A label needs a realized plan. A node is marked solved only inside the branch where strict realization returned a number. Strict realization executes the plan segment by segment on the full joint state and returns nothing on the first segment it cannot execute. A plan that cannot be played produces no label at all.
  2. Only certified subtrees propagate. The best certified result moves up the path only from a node that certified. Record extraction skips any child with no certified descendant.
  3. The greedy sibling path uses the same gate. A greedily completed sibling is passed to the same certifier. If certification fails, a counter increases and the sibling is dropped.

The value net's own prediction is written into every record, but under its own key. It is provenance. It is never the label, and nothing reads it back during training. The network does choose which plans get measured, because the policy ranks candidates and the value net drives selection. It never supplies a number that becomes an answer.

A second, independent check applies to every exam row. Before the bench runner accepts a result, the replay validator re-executes the emitted move list from the instance's start positions, using only the physics layer and the standard library. It requires that every move is a real slide that changes the robot's cell, that the sequence length equals the claimed move count, and that the target robot ends on the goal. It never touches the planner or the realizer.

So the loop can be wrong about which plan is best. It cannot be wrong about whether a plan works or about how many moves it costs.

The error that can still enter the data, and it is large in the B2 vocabulary. A label is a certified upper bound on the true cost-to-go. If the search never finds the shortest plan through a candidate, that candidate's label is too large, and if the error is uneven inside a group, the "optimal" marker can point at the wrong candidate. A second labelling mechanism exists, and the loop does not use it

The project has a certified-descent labeller, which prices a candidate by greedy network completion instead of by exhaustive solving. It produced the "twin" corpora, and a per-size supervised planner retrained on them solves 212 of 232 graded g24r4 instances at mean regret 3.047, against the exact-labelled planner's 205 of 232 at 4.220. The self-play loop never imports that labeller and writes a different label source string. One honest overlap exists: the loop's sibling-completion option uses the same idea, and every run used that option. But the resulting plan is still certified before it can carry a label. In neither mechanism does a network output become a training target.

The breakthrough

The reason a search-time change was the only remaining lever is a measurement and not an opinion. On the 232-instance graded exam the exhaustive B2 language optimum, with no network anywhere, still writes solutions 1.171 moves longer than optimal on the 228 instances it can express, and reaches the optimum on 62.7 % of them. The move-by-move planner on the same exam sits at 0.068 mean regret and 94.1 % optimal. A perfect ranker with unlimited search inside the subgoal language cannot pass 1.171. Better networks cannot pass it. Better search cannot pass it. Only a change to the set of actions can.

The change is small. Before planning, let one or two robots slide, price each resulting position cheaply, and spend part of the same expansion budget planning from the best few positions. Each slide is charged as a real move.

  1. Search the untouched position. Run the standard PUCT search on the instance as given, at budget 500, keeping the cheapest certified plan. Call the result the incumbent. Add its expansions to the running total.
  2. Enumerate every legal single slide. On the graded exam at 24x24 with 4 robots this yields a mean of 13.72 legal slides per instance, with a minimum of 8 and a maximum of 16.
  3. Score each slid position with a cheap estimator. The estimator builds the moved state, creates a two-node plan whose single edge is fixed at the exact shortest path length when the engine can compute one, repeatedly pins any remaining open edge to its exact shortest path, and returns the resulting abstract plan cost. It builds no tree, calls no network, and certifies nothing.
  4. Form the depth-1 candidate list. Each legal slide becomes a candidate with rank key equal to the estimate plus 1. The plus 1 charges the slide as a real move at ranking time. Every legal slide enters the list.
  5. Extend the most promising positions. Sort the depth-1 states by their raw estimate, take the best 4, and enumerate every legal slide from each. Each two-slide position becomes a candidate with rank key equal to its estimate plus 2. Depth 3 repeats the construction from the best 4 depth-2 states.
  6. Merge and sort. All candidates live in one list sorted by rank key, so depth-1 and depth-2 prefixes compete directly for the same slots, with the depth-2 ones carrying a plus-2 handicap instead of plus 1.
  7. Run a sub-search on the top 8 candidates, in rank order, at a budget of 80 each while budget remains. One pruning rule applies: skip a candidate whose rank key is at least 3 above the current best total. A skipped candidate still consumes one of the 8 slots, and its budget is not reassigned.
  8. Compete. A sub-search result replaces the incumbent only when the prefix length plus the sub-search's strict move count is strictly less than the current best. Ties go to the incumbent, which means to the pure-subgoal result when one exists. The winning move list is the prefix slides followed by the sub-plan's moves.
  9. Report the total spend, the composed move list, the winner tag and the four search constants.

A prefix of length p costs exactly p strict moves. The charge is applied at ranking time and again at comparison time, and the independent replay checker then counts the composed list, so the charge is enforced by a program that knows nothing about prefixes.

Verification, in two layers

Inside the search, the sub-search's certifier was constructed with the moved state, so strict realization executes the sub-plan against the position that exists after the slides. After the run, the independent replay validator reads the composed move list, replays it from the original start positions under full joint physics, and requires that each move is a real slide, that the length equals the claimed count, and that the target robot ends on the goal. Every hybrid payload in the repository has a passing replay log with zero failures: 231 of 232 rows passed on the graded exam with 1 skipped as unsolved, 188 of 200 on the unseen exam with 12 skipped, 177 of 218 on the frontier exam with 41 skipped, and the same pattern on all four transfer payloads.

Caveats on the breakthrough, all of them

  1. Most of the depth-2 gain is wider screening and not two-slide plans. The depth-2 configuration changes four things at once against depth 1. Across the three 24x24 exams, 24 of the 31 depth-2 improvements are still one-slide plans found because 8 candidates were screened instead of 6, and only 7 of 31 came from a genuine two-slide prefix. On the graded exam not one of the 10 improvements came from a two-slide prefix.
  2. The move-by-move planner still writes shorter solutions on shared instances. On the graded exam, over the 219 instances both solve, the hybrid averages 8.338 moves against 7.553, and it wins 6 while the move planner wins 64. The hybrid narrows the gap and does not close it. It does solve far more instances on fresh boards, 188 of 200 against 101 of 200.
  3. The move-by-move baseline is the original network. A re-tuned move planner gains about 5 graded and 8 frontier puzzles at a different configuration, and no re-tuned 24x24 move planner exists.
  4. The wall-clock gap is not explained by the algorithm. The payloads report 21.1 mean seconds per instance for the hybrid on the graded exam and 103.4 for the control, and that comparison is invalid. The control ran through a runner with 8 worker processes on one GPU, and the hybrid ran as a single process with 16 threads. In total job wall time the control graded run took 3,618 s for 232 instances and the hybrid's per-row seconds sum to 4,898 s. Nobody has measured the two systems at equal concurrency.
  5. The two systems cannot reach the same number of search steps, and the asymmetry runs both ways. The hybrid's structural cap is 1140 against the control's 1200, so it can never spend the full budget, and its search on the untouched position is capped at 500, which is 42 % of the control's 1200. Against that, the hybrid's average spend is higher on every exam, by 17 % on graded, 16 % on unseen and 9 % on frontier, because the control terminates early on easy instances. The graded medians are 613 expansions for the hybrid against 74.5 for the control. Only the depth-1 arm is exactly budget-matched at 600 + 6 x 100 = 1200, and its graded result is 30 shorter solutions to 0 at p = 1.9e-9, so the headline survives at an exactly equal cap.
  6. The candidate screening is free under the project's accounting and not free in reality. The estimator calls the exact shortest-path engine once per legal slide, which is 13.72 times per graded instance on average, plus once per depth-2 position. It passes no accounting handle, so these calls appear in no counter at all. The project's budget unit counts only network passes, so this is internally consistent, but a reader who thinks of the budget as "work" should know that the hybrid performs an uncounted amount of exact-engine work that the control does not.
  7. The pruning rule compares two different units. The rule tests an abstract plan cost plus prefix length against a strict move count, and its margin of 3 has no derivation in the file. A pruned candidate also consumes one of the 8 slots, so pruning reduces the number of sub-searches instead of redirecting the budget.
  8. Deduplication is partial. The seen-position set starts as the original position and grows only with depth-2 and depth-3 positions. Depth-1 positions are never added, so a two-slide prefix landing on a position also reachable in one slide is not detected as a duplicate. Nothing measures how often that happens.
  9. Ties favour the incumbent. The replacement test is a strict inequality, so a slide plan that ties the pure-subgoal plan never wins. The slide-winner counts are therefore a lower bound.
  10. The floor it beat is measured and not proved. The exhaustive probe capped 7 of the 232 graded instances, and its best-plan value is described in its own docstring as a tight upper bound rather than a proof. The paired comparison above restricts to the 225 uncapped instances to reduce this risk.
  11. The d* coverage of the unseen exam is partial. 137 of the 200 unseen instances carry an exact optimum, and the flagship solves 134 of those 137. The 1.470 figure is an average over 134 instances, not 137 and not 200.
  12. The hybrid is inference only. Generation and training were never changed. Two attempts to teach the networks about slides both failed. One was flat at two seeds. The other showed a frontier signal at one seed, 38 shorter solutions to 17, and failed to repeat it at the second seed, 28 to 29.

Second, derived numbers computed against a placeholder, which produce a plausible figure with the wrong meaning. Third, denominators that shift between the population a number was averaged over and the population it is quoted against. All four make a published number hard to reuse without reading the code.

Cannot be measured at all

  • Any exact ground truth above 64x64. The size assertion is compiled into the release build and there is no fallback. The licence argument for using a learned labeller above 64 rests on flat argmin agreement from 24 to 64, and whether it holds at 80 and 96 is by construction untestable.
  • Regret and percent optimal on any frontier set. No reference optimum exists there at any budget. On those sets solving is self-certifying and nothing else is measurable.
  • An unbiased quality comparison at the hardest rungs. Quality metrics live on the graded half, which is by definition the half an exact search finished quickly. At g24r8 that is the easiest 161 of 450 instances.

Questions the data does not settle

  • Why the B2 fidelity gauge sits in the collapse band while the exam rows improved. The loop's B2 labels agree with the exact engine on 0.451 to 0.568 of matched decisions, well inside the band the project's own calibration calls collapse, and yet the loop's exam numbers rose. Either the calibration does not transfer to the extended vocabulary, or the exact side of the comparison is itself weak because of its own caps, or the loop's gains do not depend on label fidelity. The files do not decide between these.
  • Whether the loop can ever shorten solutions. Across five B2 rounds and three mixed-size rounds, no paired move test reached significance in the loop's favour. The only two changes that moved move quality were the network rebuild, which happened before self-play, and the action-space change, which happens at inference and is not in the loop.
  • Why nothing in the training pipeline can learn about slides. The hybrid is a portfolio of independent searches with no shared tree, no slide prior and no slide value head. Both attempts to build one failed. The project's own explanation, that the networks already generalize to post-slide positions and had no gap to close, is plausible and untested.
  • Why the depth curve is nearly flat. Graded regret improves 1.009 to 0.944 to 0.861 across prefix depths 1, 2 and 3, and unseen solves stop at about 188 of 200. No deeper probe was run.
  • Why one exam disagrees on solve count. On the 8-robot frontier exam the control solves 279 of 289 against the hybrid's 276. The difference is not significant at p = 0.45, but the direction is real and unexplained.
  • Whether a chain of cold-start rounds reaches the warm-start level. The cold-start control is one round deep, and its generation still used trained networks. It shows that random weights plus one round of certified data reach the supervised baseline on fresh boards, 132 against 134 of 200. It shows nothing about round two.
  • Whether the g24r4 quality loss under the extended language is caused by its cost model. The pieces are all measured. The plans found are equal in number at 208, three times as many fail realization, both-solved solutions are 1.586 moves longer, and 15 of 28 sampled exact extended-vocabulary labels price plans that physics rejects. No experiment isolates the mechanism at that configuration.
  • Whether the by-reference construct is worth anything once a network can name it. Wired into the driver and run as a matched A/B, it produced +3 frontier solves of 134 and 0 graded solves of 316, at pooled p = 0.61. The supply was 599 candidates per graded instance and 5363 per frontier instance. The nets had never seen such a record and cannot name the referenced cell, so that result is a lower bound on the construct's value.
