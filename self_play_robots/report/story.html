From taught planners to self-play

How a Ricochet Robots planner learned to write its own lessons, and how it broke a limit that training alone could not move.

1 · the game, and why it is hard 2 · two taught planners 3 · networks that write their own training data 4 · how self-play actually works 5 · the limit that training could not move 6 · the breakthrough, step by step

This project solves Ricochet Robots puzzles with a tree search and two small neural networks. It began with two planners that an exact solver taught. It now has a planner that teaches itself.

1The game, and why it is hard

Ricochet Robots is a puzzle on a square grid with walls on some cell edges. A move sends one robot in one direction. The robot slides until a wall or another robot stops it. It cannot stop halfway. The puzzle ends when the target robot stops on the target cell, and fewer moves is better.

The sliding rule changes what a solution is. A robot cannot travel one cell at a time, so you cannot walk it to the target. The real question is what will stop it, and the other robots are the answer.

This project uses boards from 16×16 to 96×96, with 4 or 8 robots. An exact solver exists. It returns a shortest solution, but it is too slow for hard puzzles. On the fixed 24×24 benchmark of 450 puzzles it graded 232 and failed on 218, which is 48%. That split defines three exams.

  • The standard exam: the 232 puzzles it graded, where perfect play is known.
  • The hard exam: the 218 it failed, where an optimum exists but nobody knows it.
  • The new-boards exam: 200 fresh puzzles, with the optimum known for 137 of them.

Perfect play on the standard exam needs 7.69 moves per puzzle. This page scores a planner by its extra moves over perfect play, averaged over its own solves. A score of +1 means one wasted move.

2Two taught planners

The project built two kinds of planner, and the exact solver taught the networks inside both.

The move-by-move planner searches in single robot slides. Its tree is narrow and very deep, because a hard puzzle can need 30 moves. It is close to perfect when it solves, at +0.07 extra moves over its own 220 solves of the standard exam. But it spends 275 seconds per puzzle.

Its teacher fails too. On a 450-puzzle benchmark at 32×32 the exact solver failed on 275 puzzles, which is 61%. With 8 robots it failed on 289. So nobody can teach this planner at scale.

The sub-goal planner searches in decisions instead of moves. Call the robot that must travel the mover. One decision names a helper robot, the cell to send it to, and the cell where the mover will stop. It leaves two smaller puzzles behind. The tree is wide, but only 2 to 6 decisions deep. It spends 7.8 seconds per puzzle, and stays cheap as boards grow.

It pays for that speed in the plan language. Some shortest move sequences do something that no sub-goal can name. The solver-taught sub-goal planner scores +4.22 extra moves over its own 205 solves. Section 5 measures how much of that the language causes.

3Networks that write their own training data

The exact solver stops at 64×64, and below that it already fails on most puzzles. A planner learns from labels, and one label answers one decision. So where do labels come from at 96×96? A value network writes its own, at any size.

The change that makes this work is a subtraction. The older networks carried a position table, one list of 192 numbers per board cell, and it locked the saved file to one board size. The new networks carry none. Position comes from the walls, so information moves only along legal slides, and one saved file runs at every size from 16×16 to 96×96.

The planner uses two networks of this kind. The value network estimates how much more a plan will cost. The ranking network chooses which decision to try next. Together they hold 2,206,176 numbers. Modern language models hold billions.

Below 64×64 the exact solver can still grade single decisions, and the project checked the label-writing network at 20 sizes. Agreement means it picked the solver’s own best candidate. From 17×17 to 64×64 it agreed 86% to 93% of the time, at 93% on 24×24 and 90% on 64×64. That last board is four times as wide as any board it trained on.

The project retrained planners on network labels and on solver labels. At 24×24 with 4 robots the benchmark cannot tell the two retrained planners apart. On 8-robot boards the labels agreed only 82% of the time, and that planner was clearly worse. Label quality sets what a planner becomes. Without this network the story stops at 64×64.

4How self-play actually works

Fresh boards and self-made labels let the planner practise with no teacher. That is the self-play loop, and one round does seven things.

  1. Make fresh boards. The generator builds new random boards and places the robots at random. No new board may sit in an exam.
  2. Search each puzzle, with added randomness. The two networks drive a tree search, and random noise enters the ranking network’s first opinion.
  3. Replay every finished plan. The simulator plays it against the real game physics, one move at a time.
  4. Keep only the plans that work. A plan passes when the target robot really stops on the target cell. The loop discards the rest.
  5. Write the answers. At every decision, the loop lists the choices the search tried. Each choice that led to a working plan gets that plan’s real cost in moves. Choice and cost make one training answer. A lone choice teaches nothing about ranking, so the loop drops it.
  6. Retrain the two networks. Training starts from what the networks learned last round, not from scratch.
  7. Measure on fixed exams. The exams never change, and the new networks meet the old ones puzzle by puzzle.

This puzzle has no opponent and no win or loss. The outcome is a cost in moves, and fresh boards give the variety an opponent would give. Physics is the referee: does this plan work, and in how many moves?

Five rounds at 24×24 lifted the hard exam from 133 solves of 218 to a peak of 158 in round three, then settled at 153. They also cut the standard exam’s search cost from 53 expansions per puzzle to 30. One round of mixed practice then beat all five. That round drew boards of three kinds: 24×24 and 32×32 with 4 robots, and 24×24 with 8 robots. On the hard exam of each kind it reached 170 of 218, 235 of 275, and 269 of 289.

One control tested whether the loop needs a teacher at all. It began from scratch, with only its own replay-checked plans as answers. After one round it solved 132 of 200 new-board puzzles, against the solver-taught sub-goal planner’s 134. Self-play needs no teacher to start.

5The limit that training could not move

Self-play bought solve rate and speed. It never bought shorter solutions, and the plan language explains why.

A sub-goal is one word of a plan language. The base vocabulary holds one word: send a helper to a cell, so the mover stops where the plan needs it. The extended vocabulary adds three more words.

  • Park a robot aside for a later step.
  • Reuse a robot the plan already sends somewhere.
  • Use a stopper that stands in place for one move only.

Before any training, the project measured what those words can do at best. The probe lists every plan a vocabulary can express, replays each one, and compares the best with perfect play. On the standard exam the base vocabulary reaches 216 of 232 puzzles at +1.63 extra moves, and the extended vocabulary reaches 228 at +1.17. Those numbers belong to the vocabulary, not to the networks, and training cannot move them.

The measurement predicted that more practice inside the base vocabulary would buy nothing. Two rounds inside it changed nothing, so the loop moved to the extended vocabulary.

Read +1.17 as a measured limit, not a proof. The probe searched everything the extended vocabulary can express, inside a fixed search budget. Two probes at different budgets differ by a few hundredths of a move. The limit belongs to the language, so only a change to the planner’s actions can score below it.

6The breakthrough, step by step

The change is one sentence long. Before the planner starts sub-goal planning, let it play one or two ordinary robot moves first. The position after a slide is a different plan space, and it can hold a cheaper plan. Call the result the hybrid. One puzzle runs like this.

  1. Search the start position. The ordinary sub-goal search runs on the untouched puzzle, with 500 of the 1,200 expansions.
  2. List the slides. The planner lists every legal single robot move from the start.
  3. Score each one. For each slid position it builds the cheap opening plan and reads its cost.
  4. Add a second slide. The four cheapest slid positions each get one more legal move, scored the same way.
  5. Keep eight. The score adds one point per slide, and the eight cheapest starts stay.
  6. Search each kept start. Each one gets 80 expansions, so the whole search stays inside 1,200.
  7. Pay for the slides. A plan found after two slides costs two moves more than the sub-plan itself.
  8. Replay everything. Physics replays every finished plan, and the joined answers replay against the original puzzle.
  9. Return the cheapest. The cheapest total that really works wins.

The gain is the idea, not extra compute. The hybrid and the self-play sub-goal planner use the same two networks and the same cap of 1,200 expansions, and only the search differs. The hybrid solved 231 of 232 standard-exam puzzles at +0.94 extra moves, over its own 231 solves. The self-play sub-goal planner solved 230 at +1.42. On the 230 both solved, the hybrid was shorter on 40 puzzles and longer on none. The fluke chance is below one in a billion.

The hybrid also used one fifth of the running time, 21 seconds per puzzle against 103. A version that plays three moves first reaches +0.86, and the gain stops.

Two controls say this is the idea, not luck. A second, unrelated pair of networks repeated the win. The change also transfers, with no new training, to sizes it never met.

  • 174 of 175 on the 32×32 standard exam, against 172 without the change.
  • 251 of 275 on the 32×32 hard exam, against 239 without the change. There 77 of the 80 solutions that differed got shorter.
  • 276 of 289 on the 8-robot hard exam. The ordinary search solved three more there, but 82 of the 84 solutions that differed got shorter.

One limit remains. The move-by-move planner still writes shorter solutions when it solves. On the 219 standard-exam puzzles both solve, the hybrid used +0.79 more moves. That is not 0.94 minus 0.07, because those scores average over different puzzles. But that planner works one move at a time, so long solutions defeat it. On new boards it solves 101 of 200, against the hybrid’s 188.
