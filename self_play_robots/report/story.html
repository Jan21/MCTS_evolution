From taught planners to self-play

How a Ricochet Robots planner learned to teach itself, and how one change to its search broke a measured limit.

the game two taught planners networks that teach themselves the self-play loop the limit the breakthrough

This project solves Ricochet Robots puzzles with a tree search and two small neural networks. Four planners appear below.

  • The move-by-move planner picks one robot slide at a time. An exact solver taught it. It is near perfect when it solves, and very slow.
  • The solver-taught sub-goal planner plans in sub-goals instead of moves. The same solver taught it.
  • The self-play sub-goal planner is the same planner taught by its own practice, with a richer plan language.
  • The hybrid is that planner with one change to its search. It mixes ordinary robot moves with sub-goal planning, and it wins.

The game

Ricochet Robots is a puzzle on a square grid with walls on some cell edges. A move sends one robot in one direction. The robot slides until a wall or another robot stops it, so it cannot stop halfway. The puzzle ends when the target robot stops on the target cell, and fewer moves is better.

So you cannot walk a robot to the target. The real question is what will stop it in the right place, and the other robots are the answer.

An exact solver exists, but it is too slow for hard puzzles. On a fixed 24×24 benchmark it graded 232 puzzles and failed on 218. Those two sets are the standard exam and the hard exam. A new-boards exam of 200 fresh puzzles is the third.

Perfect play on the standard exam needs 7.69 moves. Every score below is extra moves over perfect play, averaged over that planner’s own solves.

Two taught planners

The move-by-move planner searches one robot slide at a time, so its tree is narrow and very deep. It is close to perfect when it solves, at +0.07 extra moves over the 220 of 232 standard-exam puzzles it reaches. But it spends 275 seconds per puzzle, and its teacher fails badly on bigger boards.

The solver-taught sub-goal planner searches in decisions instead of moves. The robot that must travel is the mover, and at the start it is the target robot. One decision names a helper robot, the cell to send it to, and the cell where the mover will then stop. That leaves two smaller puzzles, and the question repeats on each. The tree is wide but only a few decisions deep, so this planner needs 7.8 seconds per puzzle.

It pays for that speed. Every sub-goal sends a robot somewhere to help, and some shortest solutions need a move that helps nobody. This planner scores +4.22 over the 205 puzzles it solves.

Networks that teach themselves

The exact solver stops at 64×64. A planner learns from answers, and one answer settles one decision. So where do answers come from on bigger boards? A value network writes its own, at any size.

The older networks memorised a number for every square, so a file trained on one board size was useless on another. The new one reads only walls and slides, so one file runs at any size.

The planner uses two such networks. One estimates how much more a plan will cost, and the other picks the next decision to try. Across 20 board sizes the exact solver graded the answer-writing network, which chose the solver’s own best candidate 86% to 93% of the time. It never trained on a board wider than 16×16.

The self-play loop

Fresh boards and self-made answers let the planner practise with no teacher. One round does seven things.

Two steps carry the weight. The simulator replays every finished plan against the real physics. Then each choice that led to a working plan gets that plan’s true cost in moves, and choice and cost together make one answer. The search adds a little randomness, so it does not try the same thing every time. One error survives: a plan can work and still be longer than it needs to be.

The loop bought solve rate. Five rounds lifted the hard exam from 133 solves of 218 to about 153. A control that began from scratch, with no teacher, still solved 132 of 200 new boards after one round.

The limit that training could not move

Self-play never bought shorter solutions. Five rounds left the standard-exam score where it started. The solver-taught planner scores far worse, but that gap closed earlier, when the networks were rebuilt. It did not close because of the richer plan language. In that language the solver-taught planner scores slightly worse.

A sub-goal is one word of a plan language. The base vocabulary holds one word: send a helper to a cell, so the mover stops where the plan needs it. The extended vocabulary, which self-play uses, adds three.

  • A stopper may stand on a cell with no wall beside it, for one move only.
  • A robot the plan already moves may be reused where it already stands.
  • A robot standing in the way may be moved aside, and the plan replayed.

Before any training, a probe measured what those words can do at best. It lists every plan a vocabulary can express and replays each one. The extended vocabulary reaches 228 of the 232 standard-exam puzzles, and even its best plans cost +1.17 extra moves. The base vocabulary is worse still.

Read that as a measured limit, not a proof. It belongs to the plan language, so only a change to the planner’s actions can score below it.

The breakthrough, step by step

The change is one sentence long. Before the planner starts sub-goal planning, let it play one or two ordinary robot moves first. One puzzle then runs like this.

  1. Search the untouched puzzle. The sub-goal search runs unchanged, with most of the budget.
  2. List the slides. Every legal single robot move, priced by the cheapest opening plan it leads to.
  3. Keep eight, search each. A small share of the budget each, and the best four also get a second slide.
  4. Pay for the slides. A two-slide start costs two extra moves.
  5. Replay, take the cheapest. Physics replays each plan against the original puzzle, and the cheapest that works wins.

Both planners may use up to 1,200 search steps per puzzle, where one step tries one more decision. The hybrid used about a sixth more steps than the self-play sub-goal planner. It still finished five times faster, at 21 seconds per puzzle against 103. It solved 231 of 232 standard-exam puzzles at +0.94 extra moves, against the other planner’s 230 at +1.42. On the puzzles both solved, the hybrid was shorter on 40 and longer on none.

Two honest notes. Part of the gain is screening more starts, not two-slide plans. A tuning that plays three moves first reaches +0.86, but the new-boards exam stopped improving at two.

It is the idea, not luck. A second, unrelated pair of networks repeated the win. The same saved hybrid also transferred to bigger and more crowded boards, with no new training.

One limit remains. The move-by-move planner still writes shorter solutions when it solves: on the 219 puzzles both solve, the hybrid used +0.79 more moves. But it works one move at a time, so long solutions defeat it, and it solves barely half as many new boards.
