From backward-forward to self-play

How a Ricochet Robots planner learned to write its own lessons, and how it broke a limit that training alone could not move.

1 · the game 2 · two taught planners 3 · labels with no teacher 4 · one round of self-play 5 · the wall, and the key

This project solves Ricochet Robots puzzles with a tree search and two small neural networks. It began with two planners that an exact solver taught. It now has a planner that teaches itself, on boards the exact solver cannot reach. This page tells that story in order, and it reads in under ten minutes.

1The game, and why it is hard

Ricochet Robots is a puzzle on a square grid with walls on some cell edges. A move sends one robot in one direction. The robot slides in a straight line until a wall or another robot stops it. It cannot stop halfway. The puzzle ends when the target robot stops on the target cell. The score is the number of moves, and fewer is better.

The sliding rule changes what a solution is. You cannot step towards the target. The question is never which direction the goal lies in. The question is what will stop you in the right place. The other robots are the answer.

Good play spends moves to park a helper robot on an empty cell, only so the target robot can stop against it later. A solution is a small construction project, not a path.

This project uses boards from 16×16 to 96×96, with 4 or 8 robots. An exact solver exists, and its failures measure the difficulty. On the fixed 24×24 benchmark of 450 puzzles it graded 232 and failed on 218, which is 48%. That split defines the three exams used all through this page.

  • The standard exam: the 232 puzzles it graded, where perfect play is known.
  • The hard exam: the 218 it failed, where an optimum exists but nobody knows it.
  • The new-boards exam: 200 fresh puzzles, with the optimum known for 137 of them.

2Two taught planners

There are two ways to search this game. An exact solver taught the first networks for both.

The move-by-move planner searches in single robot slides. Its tree is narrow and very deep, because solutions run from about 8 to 30 moves. It is close to perfect when it solves. On the 24×24 standard exam it solves 220 of 232 puzzles at +0.07 extra moves over perfect play, averaged over its own 220 solves. But it is slow, and it spends 275 seconds per puzzle.

Its teacher fails too. On 32×32 boards the exact solver missed 61% of the benchmark, worse than the 48% at 24×24. So nobody can teach this planner at scale.

The sub-goal planner searches in decisions instead of moves. One decision reads: send helper robot H to cell S, so that the mover stops at cell B. From B the mover can finish its journey. The choice creates two new journeys, and the same question repeats on each. The tree is wide, but only 2 to 6 decisions deep. This planner spends 7.8 seconds per puzzle, and it stays cheap as boards grow.

The price is the plan language itself. Some optimal move sequences do something that no sub-goal can express. The cheapest plan the base vocabulary can express costs +1.63 extra moves over perfect play, averaged over the 216 standard-exam puzzles it reaches. An extended vocabulary costs +1.17, over 228 of the 232. Section 5 returns to this number.

3Networks that write their own training data

The exact solver stops at 64×64, and well below that size it already fails on most puzzles. A planner learns from labels, and one label answers one decision. So where do labels come from at 96×96? The project's answer is a value network that writes its own labels, at any board size.

The change that makes this work is a subtraction. The older networks each carried a learned position table, one 192-number vector per board cell. At 24×24 that table holds 110,784 numbers, and it locked the saved file to one board size. The new networks carry no position table at all. Position comes from the walls instead. Two attention masks connect cell A to cell B exactly when a robot can slide from A to B.

Information inside the network therefore moves only along legal slides. One saved file runs at 16×16, 24×24, 32×32, 64×64 and 96×96 with no change. Fresh boards are cheap too, because the generator builds a 32×32 board in 0.24 seconds and a 96×96 board in 3.8 seconds.

The planner uses two such networks. The value network estimates how much more a plan will cost. The ranking network chooses which decision to try next. Together they hold 2,206,176 numbers, which is small for a modern network.

Below 64×64 the exact solver can still grade single decisions, and the project used it at 20 board sizes. At every size from 17×17 to 64×64 the network picked the solver's own best candidate 86% to 93% of the time. The curve has no size trend, at 93% on 24×24 boards and 90% on 64×64 boards. That last board is four times the largest one the network ever trained on.

The project then retrained planners on network labels and on solver labels, over the same boards and puzzles. At 24×24 with 4 robots the benchmark cannot separate the two planners. Label quality sets what the planner becomes. On 8-robot boards the labels agreed only 82% of the time, and the planner trained on them was clearly worse. Without this network the story stops at 64×64.

4How self-play actually works

Cheap boards and self-made labels let the planner practise with no teacher at all. That practice is the self-play loop. The loop runs in rounds, and one round does seven things, in order.

  1. Make fresh boards. The generator builds brand-new random boards and places the robots at random. Every board carries an ID number, and the loop refuses to start if any ID touches an exam pool.
  2. Search each puzzle, with added randomness. The two networks drive a tree search, and one expansion is one step of that search. The code mixes random noise into the ranking network's opinion at the root of the tree. The search therefore does not always try the same thing first.
  3. Replay every finished plan. The simulator plays it against the real game physics, one move at a time.
  4. Keep only the plans that work. A plan passes when the target robot really stops on the target cell. The loop discards a plan that fails.
  5. Turn the kept plans into training answers. Every candidate with a certified plan below it gets one record, and the label is the real cost of that plan. The loop drops a decision with fewer than two labelled candidates, because one candidate cannot teach ranking.
  6. Retrain the two networks. Training starts from the previous round's weights, not from random weights.
  7. Measure on fixed exams. The exams never change, and paired tests compare the new networks against the old ones.

What cannot go wrong. A wrong answer cannot enter the training data. A plan that does not replay produces no record at all. Label corruption is therefore structurally impossible.

What can go wrong. One error survives. A plan can be legal and still longer than it needs to be. The loop can teach itself a detour, but it cannot teach itself an illegal move.

AlphaZero needs an opponent and a win or a loss. This puzzle has neither.

  • There is no opponent, and no win or loss. The outcome is a cost in moves, and fresh boards give the variety that an opponent would give.
  • Physics is the referee. The simulator answers one question: does this plan work, and in how many moves?

Five rounds at 24×24 lifted the hard exam from 133 solves to 158, and the last two rounds held it at 153. The same rounds also cut the search cost on the standard exam from 53 expansions per puzzle to 30. One round of mixed practice on three board types then beat all five. That round made 941 fresh puzzles, solved 864 of them, and certified 11,915 training records. On the hard exams it reached:

  • 170 of 218 at 24×24,
  • 235 of 275 at 32×32,
  • 269 of 289 with 8 robots.

The loop started from networks copied from the solver-taught planner, so one control tested whether it needed them. That control began from random weights and used only its own replay-checked plans as answers. After one round it solved 132 of 200 new-board puzzles, against the solver-taught planner's 134.

5The wall, and the key that opened it

Self-play bought solve rate and speed. It never bought shorter solutions, and the number from section 2 explains why.

Before any training, the project measured the plan language's own limit. The probe lists every plan the vocabulary can express, replays each one, and compares the best with perfect play. On the standard exam the base vocabulary reaches 216 of the 232 puzzles, and its best plans cost +1.63 extra moves. The extended vocabulary reaches 228 puzzles and costs +1.17. Those numbers belong to the vocabulary, not to the networks. Training cannot move them.

The measurement also made a prediction. Two rounds of self-play inside the base vocabulary changed nothing on any exam. The loop then moved to the extended vocabulary.

Read the floor as a measured limit, not a mathematical proof. The probe searched everything the vocabulary can express, under stated budgets of search and time. The two deepest probes differ by a few hundredths of a move. The hybrid beat the extended floor by a quarter of a move, which is far more.

The key is a change to the search, not to the networks. Let the planner play one or two ordinary robot moves before it starts sub-goal planning. The position after one slide is a different plan space. It can hold a cheaper plan, even after the slide costs its own move.

With two moves allowed first, the flagship reaches +0.94 extra moves, averaged over its own 231 solves. With three, it reaches +0.86. Both sit below the extended vocabulary's floor of +1.17, which the probe measured over the 228 puzzles it proved reachable. A planner that slides first is no longer a pure sub-goal planner.

The gain is the idea, not extra compute. Both searches used the same two networks and the same cap of 1,200 expansions. On the 230 puzzles both searches solved, the hybrid found a shorter solution on 40 and a longer one on none. It also needed one fifth of the running time. The fluke chance is below one in a billion.

Two limits remain. The move-by-move planner still wins on pure solution quality. On the 219 standard-exam puzzles that both planners solved, the flagship used +0.79 more moves than the move-by-move planner. But that planner works one move at a time, so long solutions defeat it. On the new-boards exam it solves 101 of 200, against the flagship's 188. Work on boards beyond the exact solver's reach is in progress, and physics replay will be the only referee there.
