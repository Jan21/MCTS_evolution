From taught planners to self-play

How a Ricochet Robots planner learned to teach itself, and how one change to its search broke a measured limit.

1 · the game 2 · two taught planners 3 · networks that teach themselves 4 · the self-play loop 5 · the limit 6 · the breakthrough

This project solves Ricochet Robots puzzles with a tree search and two small neural networks. Four planners appear on this page.

  • The move-by-move planner picks one robot slide at a time. It is near perfect when it solves, and very slow.
  • The solver-taught sub-goal planner plans in sub-goals instead of moves. An exact solver taught it.
  • The self-play sub-goal planner is the same kind of planner, taught by its own practice, with a richer plan language.
  • The hybrid is that planner with one change to its search. It mixes ordinary robot moves with sub-goal planning, and it is the best planner here.

1The game

Ricochet Robots is a puzzle on a square grid with walls on some cell edges. A move sends one robot in one direction. The robot slides until a wall or another robot stops it, so it cannot stop halfway. The puzzle ends when the target robot stops on the target cell, and fewer moves is better.

So you cannot walk a robot to the target. The real question is what will stop it in the right place, and the other robots are the answer.

An exact solver exists, but it is too slow for hard puzzles. On a fixed 24×24 benchmark of 450 puzzles it graded 232 and failed on 218. That split gives the standard exam of 232 puzzles and the hard exam of 218. A new-boards exam of 200 fresh puzzles is the third.

Perfect play on the standard exam needs 7.69 moves. Every score below is extra moves over perfect play, averaged over that planner’s own solves.

2Two taught planners

The move-by-move planner searches one robot slide at a time, so its tree is narrow and very deep. It is close to perfect when it solves, at +0.07 over its own 220 solves. But it spends 275 seconds per puzzle, and its teacher fails at scale: at 32×32 the exact solver graded only 175 of 450 puzzles.

The solver-taught sub-goal planner searches in decisions instead of moves. The robot that must travel is the mover, and at the start it is the target robot. One decision names a helper robot, the cell to send it to, and the cell where the mover will then stop. That leaves two smaller puzzles, and the same question repeats on each. The tree is wide but only 2 to 6 decisions deep, so this planner needs 7.8 seconds per puzzle.

It pays for that speed in its plan language. Some shortest move sequences do something no sub-goal can name, and it scores +4.22 over its own 205 solves.

3Networks that teach themselves

The exact solver stops at 64×64. A planner learns from answers, and one answer settles one decision. So where do answers come from at 96×96? A value network writes its own, at any size.

The older networks memorised a number for every square, so a file trained on one board size was useless on another. The new one reads only walls and slides, so one file runs at any size.

The planner uses two such networks, holding 2,206,176 numbers between them. One estimates how much more a plan will cost, and the other chooses which decision to try next. The exact solver graded the answer-writing network at 20 board sizes, from 17×17 to 64×64. It picked the solver’s own best candidate 86% to 93% of the time. It never trained on a board wider than 16×16.

4The self-play loop

Fresh boards and self-made answers let the planner practise with no teacher. One round does seven things.

Two steps carry the weight. The simulator replays every finished plan against the real game physics. Then each choice that led to a working plan gets that plan’s true cost in moves, and choice and cost together make one answer. One error survives: a plan can work and still be longer than it needs to be.

The loop bought solve rate. Five rounds lifted the hard exam from 133 solves of 218 to a peak of 158, then it settled at 153. On new boards the seed networks solved 175 of 200 and the practised ones 182. A control that began from scratch, with no teacher at all, still solved 132 after one round.

5The limit that training could not move

Self-play never bought shorter solutions. Before the first round the planner scored +1.55 extra moves on the standard exam, and after five rounds it still scored +1.55. The solver-taught sub-goal planner scores +4.22, and the self-play one scores +1.42. That fall happened before self-play began, and it came from rebuilding the networks, not from the richer plan language. In that language the solver-taught sub-goal planner scores +4.89, worse than its +4.22.

A sub-goal is one word of a plan language. The base vocabulary holds one word: send a helper to a cell, so the mover stops where the plan needs it. The extended vocabulary, which self-play uses, adds three more.

Before any training, a probe measured what those words can do at best. It lists every plan a vocabulary can express, replays each one, and compares the best with perfect play. The base vocabulary reaches 216 of the 232 standard-exam puzzles at +1.63, and the extended vocabulary reaches 228 at +1.17. Read +1.17 as a measured limit, not a proof. It belongs to the plan language, so only a change to the planner’s actions can score below it.

6The breakthrough, step by step

The change is one sentence long. Before the planner starts sub-goal planning, let it play one or two ordinary robot moves first. One puzzle then runs like this.

  1. Search the untouched puzzle. The sub-goal search runs unchanged, with 500 of the 1,200 search steps.
  2. List the slides. Every legal single robot move, priced by the cheapest opening plan it leads to.
  3. Keep eight, search each. Eighty steps each, and the best four also get a second slide.
  4. Pay for the slides. A two-slide start costs two extra moves.
  5. Replay, take the cheapest. Physics replays each plan against the original puzzle, and the cheapest that works wins.

The hybrid and the self-play sub-goal planner use the same two networks and the same cap of 1,200 search steps. The hybrid used about 17% more of that cap, and still finished in one fifth of the time. It solved 231 of 232 standard-exam puzzles at +0.94 over its own 231 solves, against 230 at +1.42. On the 230 both solved, the hybrid was shorter on 40 puzzles and longer on none. The fluke chance is below one in a billion.

Two honest notes. Part of the gain is screening more starts, rather than two-slide plans: only 5 of 46 slide wins on new boards used both slides. A setting that plays three moves first reaches +0.86, but the new-boards exam stopped improving at two.

It is the idea, not luck. A second, unrelated pair of networks repeated the win. The same saved hybrid, with no new training, also beat the self-play sub-goal planner at 32×32. It solved 174 of the 175 puzzles the exact solver graded there, against 172. It solved 251 of the 275 it failed, against 239, and was shorter on 77 of the 80 puzzles where the two differed.

One limit remains. The move-by-move planner still writes shorter solutions when it solves: on the 219 standard-exam puzzles both solve, the hybrid used +0.79 more moves. But it works one move at a time, so long solutions defeat it, and it solves 101 of 200 new boards against the hybrid’s 188.
