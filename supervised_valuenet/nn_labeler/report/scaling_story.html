Teaching a network to be the teacher

Campaign complete — every run in

A plain-English account of the scaling experiments · 2026-08-16 · local working document

We build programs that solve a sliding-robot puzzle, and they learn from millions of worked examples produced by an exact solver — a program that always finds a shortest solution but can take a very long time. On big boards that solver runs out of breath, and one planned batch of examples would have eaten nearly half the project's entire computer budget. So we trained a network to produce the examples instead: it learned on the smallest boards we have, and it still judges boards more than three times larger almost as well.

The puzzle, in one minute

Picture a square grid with a scattering of walls. On it stand a handful of robots. You pick a robot and a direction, and it slides — like a curling stone on ice — until something stops it: a wall, the edge of the board, or another robot. It never stops in the middle of open floor. One robot has a target square, and your job is to get it there in as few slides as possible.

That one rule is what makes the puzzle hard. You usually cannot send the target robot straight to its square, because nothing is there to stop it. You first have to park a different robot on the far side of the target as a bumper — and parking that robot may need a bumper of its own. Good solutions look like small chains of set-up moves followed by one clean run home.

Our system is a planner: a program that searches through possible move sequences, with two neural networks advising the search about which branch looks promising. Everything below is about where those networks get their training material.

One board and one move: the robot slides in a straight line until a wall or another robot stops it, which is why helper robots have to be parked first.

Why we need labeled examples

The networks learn by example, and each example is narrow and specific. It says: here is a half-finished plan, and here is one candidate next step — if you take this step, how many moves are still needed to finish? That number is the label. Practitioners call it the cost-to-go; it simply means how many moves are left to finish.

Collect millions of those little answers and a network can learn to rank candidate steps without searching: shown a position and a list of options, it says which option leaves the least work. That ranking is what makes the planner fast, because it lets the search skip the branches that go nowhere.

Until now, every single one of those answers came from the exact solver. To label one candidate, you hand the solver the half-finished plan with that candidate glued on and ask it for the shortest completion. The label is exactly right by construction. It is also, on a big board, extremely expensive — and that is the whole problem.

The teacher is running out of breath

The exact solver's cost explodes in two directions at once: bigger boards and more robots. With four robots on a 16×16 board it answers every question we ask, within its effort cap, 100% of the time. Add robots and it starts failing: it hits the cap and returns nothing on 29.8% of questions at six robots and 40.9% at eight. Grow the board instead and the same thing happens: 48.4% failures on 24×24 with four robots, 64.2% on 24×24 with eight, 61.1% on 32×32 with four. On the boards we care most about, the teacher is silent more often than not.

How often the exact solver simply fails to answer inside its effort cap, as boards grow and robots multiply.

The price of the answers it does give is the other half of the wall. One planned labeling campaign, on an extended set of moves, was projected at about 441 node-hours. A node-hour is one hour of one whole eight-GPU machine; the project's entire grant is 1000 of them, and roughly 879 were left at the time. One batch of training data would have consumed nearly half of what remained.

We tried the obvious cheap fix: cap the solver's effort and take whatever it manages. That ran in about 5 node-hours — and quietly ruined the data. The examples that take the solver longest are precisely the interesting ones, so capping it deleted them: the key example type fell from 13.5% of the data to 4.8%. A network retrained on the capped data lost 24.6 percentage points on the hardest benchmark. The workaround did not shrink the job; it shrank the lesson.

The idea: one network that fits every board

If the exact solver cannot scale, the network has to label its own future training data. For that to work, one network has to run on boards of any size — and ours could not. It read the board as a collection of squares, each square a separate piece of input, wired to the other squares by where a robot could slide from one to another. That wiring already encodes the board's geometry, and it does not care how big the board is.

Exactly one piece did care: a learned position table, a lookup with one row per square that told the network where each square sat. One row per square means the table only exists for one board size, and the whole network was locked to that size by it.

The network sees each square as its own input and connects squares by where a robot could slide between them, so no part of it depends on how big the board is.

We tried three replacements: a position signal computed from each square's coordinates by formula, extra input channels carrying the coordinates directly, and — as the control — nothing at all. The surprise was that nothing at all won, and won clearly. The slide connections apparently tell the network everything it needs about where things are. Removing the position signal entirely is also the most portable choice imaginable: there is now no part of the network that knows, or needs to know, the size of the board it is looking at.

Start tiny, fail cheap

Before spending real money we built a practice bed out of boards small enough that the exact solver is instant: eight sizes from 8×8 to 15×15, four robots each, 1200 boards apiece. Labeling 1050 boards at each size took between 15 and 56 seconds — ordinary CPU work on a login machine, no GPU, no queue, no cost worth counting.

Two things broke, and that was the point

The first training round put eight variants on one GPU for six hours and eleven minutes — 0.77 node-hours. Seven of the eight learned nothing at all. They landed in a lazy corner where the network predicts the same number for every candidate, which scores about as well as guessing and is much easier to reach than actually learning. The giveaway was almost comic: three genuinely different network designs reported the same best score, 1.9154, down to the last digit. Different networks cannot agree to four decimals. They had all stopped predicting anything, so the score was no longer measuring them.

Twenty rounds of training, healthy run against collapsed run: one steadily improves, the other never leaves a flat line near where it started.

The cause was a missing warm-up. The original training recipe started each network on the easiest decisions and only later mixed in the hard ones — a curriculum, the same trick as teaching addition before algebra. Our rewritten version had dropped it. The single healthy run had been handed a naturally easy-first dataset by accident, which is why it alone survived. With the curriculum put back, everything trained.

The second break was sneakier. Our first attempt at the curriculum quietly disabled the training framework's own progress checks: the framework decides when to test the network by counting how many batches an epoch should contain, our curriculum handed back fewer than it had promised, and so the end-of-epoch test never fired. No error, no warning — just a run producing no measurements and saving no networks. A seven-minute health check on the log caught it, and the job was killed after 40 minutes, costing 0.08 node-hours. The fix was to stop being clever: run the easy phase and the full phase as two straightforward training passes, so every count is honest by construction.

Both failures now have automatic detectors. A collapsed network is flagged by a spread measure that goes to zero when predictions stop varying, and the training script exits with an error if its progress checks are missing. Total cost of both lessons: under one node-hour, all of it on the smallest boards we own.

How far does it reach?

The rerun of the second training round, five and a half hours for 0.69 node-hours, settled the design questions. Every no-position-signal variant trained healthily, and two runs started from different random beginnings agreed to within 0.03 extra moves everywhere — the result is not luck. The formula-based position signal stayed untrainable even with the curriculum, confirming that leaving it out was not just simpler but better. And training on a mix of sizes (8, 9 and 10) beat training on any single size at every size we tested.

Then the real question: how far does a network trained only on 8×8 through 10×10 boards actually reach? We measured its label error — how many extra moves it is off by, on average — on fresh boards at every size up to 32×32, and how often its top-ranked candidate really is an optimal step.

Average label error and top-choice accuracy from the three sizes the network trained on out to boards more than three times larger, with no cliff anywhere.

At its training sizes it is off by 0.16 to 0.21 extra moves and picks an optimal step 91–92% of the time. At sizes it never saw, it degrades gently and steadily: 0.23 at 11×11, 0.30 at 13×13, 0.30 at 15×15, all around 89–90% optimal picks. At 16×16 with six robots — a bigger board and more robots than it ever saw — 0.47 extra moves and 85%. At 24×24, 0.50 and 87%. At 32×32, a board 3.2 times wider than anything in its training data, it is off by 0.71 extra moves on average and still picks an optimal next step 81% of the time. There is no cliff on this curve, only a slope.

One scare along the way deserves telling. An early measurement at 16×16 read 3.39 extra moves — a catastrophic number that looked exactly like the extrapolation collapse we feared. It was not the board size. That particular 16×16 dataset was an old one, built by a different and now-retired board generator, so the network was being tested on boards unlike anything in its world. Re-measured on data from the current generator, the same network scored 0.47. We very nearly drew a sweeping conclusion from a mismatched file.

For scale: the hand-written scoring rule the planner used before any networks existed is off by 0.72, 0.85, 0.93 and 1.18 extra moves at sizes 8, 9, 10 and 12. The trained network is about 3.5 times more accurate — and its worst measurement anywhere, at 32×32, is still better than the old rule's best.

Making labels we can trust

Judging candidates well is not the same as producing training data. For that the network has to put a number on each candidate, and the number has to mean something. Here is how a label is made without the exact solver.

Take a decision point with, say, a dozen candidate next steps. Commit to one of them. Then let the network finish the plan greedily — at each step it names the option it likes best, and we take it, no searching, no backtracking, straight to a finished plan. Now the crucial part: that finished plan is translated back into actual robot moves and replayed on the actual board under the actual rules of the game. If every slide lands where the plan said it would and the target robot ends on its square, the plan is real, and its length becomes the label for that candidate. If the replay fails, the candidate gets no label at all.

How one label is made without the exact solver: commit to a candidate, let the network finish the plan greedily, then replay the finished plan under the game's real physics.

This gives labels a useful one-sided honesty. A greedy finish may not be the shortest finish, so a label can say "nine moves" where seven were possible — it can be pessimistic. But it can never be fictional, because a plan that does not play out is thrown away rather than recorded. Wrong-but-real is a bias we can measure and correct; invented numbers would poison everything downstream.

So we measured the pessimism. On boards small enough for the exact solver, we generated labels both ways and compared them candidate by candidate.

About 85% of labels are exactly the optimal number. The typical label is not off at all — the middle of the distribution is a gap of zero, and nine labels in ten are off by at most one move. On the candidates that are genuinely the best move available, where being right matters most, accuracy is higher still, above 90% at all three sizes. The bar was set in advance at an average gap of half a move or less; all three sizes pass. The cost of this check was 58 minutes, 0.12 node-hours.

The honest caveat: the labeler keeps about two thirds of the candidates it is offered. The other third produce no finished, replayable plan and are dropped. That is a yield cost — we get fewer examples per board — not a correctness cost, and dropping is exactly the behaviour we want when the alternative is guessing.

Does it save computer time?

The truthful answer has three parts, and only two of them favour the network.

On easy settings the exact solver still wins. Small boards, few robots, the basic move set: a fast rewritten version of the solver labels a thousand boards in under a minute, as the practice bed above shows. Nothing about a neural network beats that, and we are not pretending otherwise. Those rungs stay exactly labeled.

Where the solver's cost explodes, the network wins. That is the 441-node-hour class of job — the one whose only affordable workaround silently destroyed the data it was meant to produce. Network labeling turns that into GPU work whose cost grows with the number of examples rather than with the difficulty of each one.

Where the solver fails outright, the network is the only option. On the hardest 32×32 puzzles the exact solver already returns nothing 61.1% of the time. There is no price at which it produces those labels; the question is not "cheaper?" but "at all?"

The capstone: labeling boards three times too big

The production network — call it v1 — trained with a twist worth telling. It learned well for two full rounds, then in round three fell into the same "answer the same number for everything" trap that bit us at the start, and stayed stuck for the remaining 24 rounds. Nothing was lost: the training system always keeps the best network it has seen, which was a healthy one, and the detector we added after the first incident flagged the collapse in the log without anyone having to go looking. Two fixes went in the same day — training now gives up after three stuck rounds instead of burning hours, and a false alarm in the older check was corrected.

Then the decisive test. The saved network labeled 24×24 and 32×32 boards — up to 3.2 times bigger than anything it trained on — and every label was checked against the exact solver's own answer:

Board sizeLabels exactly right Picks the same best stepAverage gap Impossible labels
24×2485.2%92.6%0.57 moves3 of 3,522
32×3287.7%91.5%0.55 movesnone

It works. Nearly nine labels in ten are exactly the number the exact solver would have written, and when the network has to choose the best next step — the thing a labeler is really for — it agrees with the exact solver more than nine times in ten. Notably it agrees better on these huge boards than on the small ones it was tested on earlier: its sense of which step is best travels, even as its sense of the exact number drifts a little.

But it does not save time here. We measured both on the same boards. The exact solver — the fast rewritten one — labels 200 puzzle situations at 32×32 in 15 seconds on four ordinary processor cores. The network needs minutes of graphics-card time for the same work: between 9 and 250 times more, depending on how the work is set up. On these puzzles, in this simplified move language, the old tool is simply better.

So where does the network earn its keep? In the richer move language the project actually cares about, where the exact solver's cost exploded to a projected 441 node-hours — roughly half the project's entire computing grant — and where the shortcut that made it affordable quietly deleted the very examples it was supposed to produce. The network has no such shortcut available to it: it walks a fixed number of steps and stops, so the trade between cost and quality never arises.

The student that aced the test but flubbed the job

We then trained a second production network, v2 — same recipe, same data, but smaller steps while learning, which is the standard remedy for the instability that hurt v1. It worked: v2 trained all the way through without ever collapsing.

It also beat v1 on every practice exam we gave it. The practice exam is simple: show the network a decision point where we already know the right answer, and count how often its top-ranked candidate really is the best one. v2 won at every board size, by one to three points, with smaller numerical errors too.

Then we made both networks actually do the job — run the whole labeling pipeline end to end and compare the finished labels with the exact solver's answers. v1 won.

The margins in the bottom half are small — 0.2 to 1.2 points, one run each — so the honest sentence is "v2 is no better where it counts", not "v2 is bad". But the lesson is worth stating plainly: a better score on the measuring stick does not guarantee better real output. Which is exactly why we decided in advance that the real-output comparison, not the practice exam, would pick the winner. Had we promoted on the exam score, everything downstream would now be built from the labels of the weaker network. v1 keeps the job, and the whole campaign now runs on one network.

Tearing down the wall

Every board used to arrive with a huge pre-computed table attached: for every square, how far it is from every other square. It is like printing a phone book of every possible route through a town before you move in. Useful — but you pay for the whole book up front, and the book grows brutally with the town. At 32×32 the printing took 222 seconds per board. At 96×96 it would have taken about twelve hours per board. That, not the network, was what stopped us going bigger.

So we stopped printing the book. Boards now answer distance questions when something asks, and remember the answers — a map app instead of a phone book. Nothing is computed until it is needed, and nothing is computed twice.

The obvious worry is whether the answers changed. They cannot, for structural reasons we could argue on paper — but "cannot" is cheap, so we checked it instead: we ran the entire labeling pipeline both ways on the same boards with the real network in the loop and compared the output. All 69 output records came out byte-for-byte identical, and so did every intermediate list of candidates, in the same order.

Board sizeOld way: print the phone bookNew way: ask as needed

The honest overhead: one individual distance question now takes about 1.4 times longer to answer than a table lookup did — irrelevant next to not building the table at all. Board sizes 40 through 96 went from "a research problem" to "ordinary work" in an afternoon.

Every single step of the ladder

The project's original question was a ladder: can the network label 17×17? Then 18×18? And every whole size in between, all the way to 32×32? We had answered the two ends and skipped the middle, because preparing those boards the old way meant days of queueing and more than ten resubmissions. With the wall down, every missing rung fit in a single job of 1 hour 17 minutes.

The result is that nothing happens. Quality is flat. Across all sixteen sizes the network's labels pick the same best next step as the exact solver between 86.4% and 92.6% of the time, with no downward slide as boards grow — if anything the top of the range sits at the big end. There is no cliff anywhere between the sizes it learned on and the biggest board we can still check.

One crash, one better experiment

The experiment meant to prove the network saves real money — the richer move language, the 441-node-hour bill — crashed after 91 seconds. Out of memory.

The reason is worth stating because it caught us out. Teaching a network on the biggest boards costs far more memory than using it on them. To learn, a network must keep everything it computed so it can work backwards and adjust itself; to merely answer, it can forget each step as it goes. We had been running 32×32 boards through networks for weeks. We had never tried to teach on one.

The fix made the experiment better rather than worse. The network now trains only on boards up to 24×24 and labels the 32×32 boards having never seen one — which is the project's whole trick anyway, and it removes a quiet unfairness in the old design, where the network would have studied the very boards it was about to be graded on. Cost of the crash: 91 seconds, about a thousandth of a node-hour. It then trained healthily, and the number to watch was whether the example type the old shortcut deleted would come back at its natural rate of about 13% instead of the degraded 5%. (It did not. That story is a few chapters below.)

The experiment that decides the headline

Everything so far says the network's labels look like the exact solver's. The last experiment asked the question that actually settles it: do planners taught from them come out as good?

It is a twin study. Take exactly the same boards and exactly the same puzzles the original planners learned from. Change one thing only: who wrote the answer key — the exact solver, or our network. Train identical planners on each pile. Test both on the identical held-out benchmark, which neither has ever seen. If the network-taught planners solve just as many puzzles, then network-made training data really can replace exact-solver data. That is the claim this whole project is built toward.

Three settings: 24×24 with four robots, 24×24 with eight robots, and 32×32. The labeling network never trained on any of them.

One honest cost note. Writing the network's answer keys turned out about twelve times slower than our first estimate. The earlier checks only had to grade the first move of each puzzle; a real answer key means walking each solution the whole way to the end. The three piles took roughly two days, running on three graphics cards at once.

The answer, in three doses

All three settings finished, and the answer came back better than a plain yes. It came back as a dose-response — the shape a medical trial produces when a drug works at the full dose, half-works at a smaller one, and fails at a smaller one still. Here the dose is fidelity: how often the network's answer key names the same best next step as the exact solver.

At 91% agreement — 24×24 with four robots — the two sides are indistinguishable, and getting to that word took some discipline. We trained two planners from each answer key, four in all, because a planner's final score wobbles depending on the random numbers it happens to start from. The wobble turned out to be the whole story of our first result: the first network-taught planner beat the exact-taught one, 91.4% of puzzles solved against 88.4%, and for a day it looked as though the network were somehow the better teacher. The repeat run, identical but for those starting random numbers, scored 87.9%. The two network-taught runs straddle the exact-taught pair, which sat steady at 88.4% both times. So the honest reading is equivalence and the exciting reading was luck of the draw. We demoted it ourselves.

At 89% agreement — 32×32 — the network-taught planner solved just as many puzzles: 84.6% against 84.0%. On the hardest set, the puzzles nothing had ever solved before, it was nominally ahead too, 48.0% against 46.2%. What slipped was polish. Its solutions were the shortest possible 45.9% of the time against the exact-taught planner's 51.7%: it arrives as often, and wanders a little more on the way.

At 82% agreement — 24×24 with eight robots, a crowded setting the labeling network had studied least — it fell apart: 68.3% solved against 89.4%. That is not a wobble, it is a collapse, and the tell was visible during training. The network-taught planner's best moment was its very first round out of thirty, and it never improved on it; the exact-taught one kept improving to the last round. That is what a learner does when the material it is given is too muddled to learn from.

The dose-response: answer-key fidelity along the bottom, the planner it produces up the side. Nine agreements in ten is as good as gold; eight in ten is not a smaller benefit but no benefit at all.

The moral fits in a sentence. Answer keys do not have to be perfect, but there is a cliff: around nine agreements in ten they are as good as gold, a little below that the solutions get slightly scruffier, and well below it learning breaks outright.

Two caveats we owe the reader. The 32×32 and eight-robot rows are one run each, so their small margins carry the same wobble that fooled us once already — though no wobble we have ever measured is anywhere near twenty-one points. And the three settings differ in more than fidelity: the harder ones also yield smaller answer keys, because anything the network cannot certify is thrown away. Which ingredient does the damage is not separable from three points alone.

So we built the missing points. A day after the campaign closed, we took the 24×24 exact answer key, shrank it to the same size the network's twin had been, and then deliberately spoiled it — flipping the best-move choice to the runner-up in a measured fraction of near-tie decisions, imitating exactly how the network itself errs — to three doses: untouched (the pure size test), 86%, and 82.2%, the eight-robot collapse's dose but now on a four-robot board with everything else held fixed. If the 82% key breaks its planner the way the eight-robot one did, fidelity is proven to be the cause and the threshold is real. If it does not, the collapse was about eight robots, not about the key. We wrote both readings down before the jobs ran; either is worth having. Those results, and a second run of the 32×32 twin to test its small margin, appear in the results page's causality tab when they land.

The experiment that failed, and why that's fine

The richer-move-language experiment — the one meant to prove the network saves real money — came back negative. The special moves the whole thing was about, the ones that refer to another robot instead of naming a direction, made up 1.3% of the network's output on one setting and 0.3% on the other. We were aiming for 13.5%. Even the crude capped shortcut, the one we called ruined, managed about 5%.

The reason is the one we should have seen coming. The network could only learn from data that already existed, and the only data that existed was the capped data — the data whose special moves the cap had already deleted. A copy of a damaged original inherits the damage. A network that rarely ranks those moves highly will rarely pick them, cap or no cap. We had removed the search budget that caused the damage and left the damaged teaching material in place.

So the money-saving claim for that experiment is dead, and we say so plainly. Cost: about four node-hours and a negative entry in the log. The headline result above does not rest on it — it never did, which is why it was the right thing to bet the campaign on. The one visible rescue would be a small batch of undamaged examples from the exact solver to learn from; noted for the owner, not attempted.

To the edge of the checkable world, and past it

With the size wall gone, the ladder simply kept going: 40, then 48, 56 and 64. Agreement with the exact solver at each rung: 90.7%, 90.6%, 89.6%, 90.1%. Flat the whole way, and still inside the same band as every size from 17 up.

64×64 matters more than the others, because that is where the exact solver stops for good. Past it, nothing can grade an answer at any price. So 64 is the last board size at which anyone, ever, can check this network's work — and it passed there at exactly the fidelity it shows everywhere else. That is what licensed the next step.

Then we took it: boards at 80×80 and 96×96. There are 1,350 labeled examples at 80 and 1,031 at 96, and every single plan behind them was replayed move by move on the real board and confirmed to actually work. Not one timed out. That physical check needs no solver at all — you either slide the robots and land on the target square or you do not. What can never be known is the other half: whether a plan that works is also the shortest one. At these sizes that is unmeasurable by construction, today and forever. Their only warranty is the flat line below them, and we mark them as such wherever they are stored.

Two footnotes. The frontier does thin as boards grow: we keep about half the puzzles we attempt at 80×80 and a bit over a quarter at 96×96, the rest producing no certifiable plan and being dropped. And 96×96 is six times as wide as the biggest board the network ever studied — a board whose preparation was projected, under the old scheme, at twelve hours apiece. It now takes about four seconds.

Where we are

Updated 2026-08-17 — campaign complete; the causality check is in flight

Done. Every job of the campaign has run; four short follow-up jobs (the spoiled-key doses and one repeat run) are queued. The headline holds: planners taught from network-written answer keys are as good as planners taught from the exact solver's, provided the keys agree with it about nine times in ten — and below that, they are not. The ladder is flat from 17 to 64, the last size anything can be checked at, and labeled boards now exist at 80 and 96. The whole thing came to roughly 27 node-hours — under 3% of the project's grant — against the 441 the old exact-solver plan projected for one batch of training data.

The last run came home. Everything above replaced the answer key alone, on boards and puzzles the exact solver's world had made. In the final experiment the network made the boards, made the puzzles and wrote the answers — no exact solver anywhere — and the planner trained on that world scored 83.4% against the exact-taught planner's 84.0% on the same fixed exam, landing between the exact and label-swap arms on answer quality (47.9% perfectly optimal vs 51.7% and 45.9%). The pipeline also turned out more generous than the label-swap experiments suggested: writing its own puzzles instead of replaying the solver's, it kept 73,202 training examples where the swap had kept 39,053. The story closes where it aimed: a solver-free pipeline, end to end, indistinguishable in the way that matters.
