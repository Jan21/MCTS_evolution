Measurement report · Ricochet Robots planner study

Planning with subgoals, or planning move by move?

Two neural-network planners solve the same sliding-robot puzzles under identical rules and search budgets — one thinks one move at a time, the other in goal-directed subgoals. This report follows the comparison from a 16×16 training ground to 32×32 boards that have outgrown exact search, and reports every result from its machine-readable source.

the game

The puzzle: robots that cannot stop

Ricochet Robots is played on a walled grid. A robot moves by sliding in a straight line and cannot stop mid-slide — it keeps going until it hits a wall or another robot, stopping in the cell just before the obstacle. One robot is the target; one cell is the goal. The task: get the target robot onto the goal cell in as few moves as possible.

What makes it hard: a robot can only stop where something blocks it. Solutions therefore hinge on parking helper robots at exact spots first so that later slides bump into them and stop where needed — a chain of preparations for one final slide. In the schematic, Yellow can only reach its goal because Blue happens to sit one cell below it; Red, with nothing in its row, slides all the way to the border.

the contestants

Two ways to build a planner

Move-by-move (the “forward” planner)

Thinks exactly like the game: “which robot slides which way right now?” — one move at a time from the start position toward the goal. Every decision is a single legal move; a solution emerges after as many decisions as the solution has moves.

Subgoals (the “backward” planner)

Thinks from the goal outward: “for the final slide to stop on the goal, a robot must first be parked HERE; getting one there needs THIS first.” Each decision is a subgoal — “park helper H on support cell S so a slider stops on cell B” — worth several moves at once, so complete plans are only a few decisions deep.

Same machinery, different vocabulary. Both planners use a proposal network (suggests what to try next), a value network (estimates how close each option is to done), and best-first search over the proposals, capped at a fixed number of search steps. One search step means: take the most promising position or partial plan, run the networks once, and generate its candidate continuations — so a step costs the same in both families and budgets are directly comparable. The study's question: which vocabulary — moves or subgoals — plans better, and which keeps working as puzzles grow?

ground rules

What keeps the comparison honest

1 · A plan must actually play

A puzzle counts as solved only if the plan plays out legally move by move on the real board. Plans that are complete on paper but bounce off robots that are not really there count as failures. This rule alone rewrote the study's early history (see the base-scale tab).

2 · Same puzzles, same budgets

Every head-to-head runs on a pinned benchmark of 450 puzzles per configuration, at the same cap of 1,200 search steps and the same proposal width (top 5). Nothing is tuned per puzzle.

3 · No exact solver at solve time

An exact solver (“the oracle”) is used only to create training labels and reference optima for scoring — never during solving. Where the oracle itself fails, solving is self-certifying: a plan that plays out legally to the goal proves itself.

4 · Only real moves are compared

Solution quality is measured in played-out moves against the oracle's move optimum. The subgoal planner's internal plan costs are never compared to move optima. On puzzles beyond the oracle's reach no optimum exists, so only solve rate, search steps and time are reported there.

Three qualifications, stated here rather than in a footnote. (1) On gradable sets the move-by-move planner still holds a real edge at 16×16 · 6 robots and 24×24 · 8 robots; at 16×16 · 8 robots the two are at parity — 262 versus 261 puzzles is a one-puzzle margin that a paired test cannot separate from noise — with the subgoal planner using 7× fewer search steps. (2) The beyond-oracle margins are matched-budget results. Give the move-by-move planner 4–5× the search budget and it gains 12–31 points at every rung probed, so "it has stopped working" is not supportable; what is supportable is that it needs several times the search to approach a rate the subgoal planner reaches immediately, and still does not catch up. (3) Both systems are single-seed everywhere, so no small difference here is defensible against seed variation, which is unmeasured.

The subgoal planner's remaining handicaps are honest and measured: longer solutions where no optimum exists, and a gap between what its extended plan language permits and what its current networks reach — a training gap, with retraining in progress.

how the subgoal planner earned its score

From 53% honest to 96%: repairs, then checking, then vocabulary

The subgoal planner's historical “99.6% solved” counted plans complete in its own notation that could not be played. Forced to play every plan out it scored 53.1% — the honest starting point. Everything after that is measured recovery.

Then: check playability inside the search

Instead of discovering at the end that a finished plan cannot be played, every partial plan is physics-checked as it is built and doomed branches are dropped immediately. An A/B run proved the check discards nothing it should keep (zero false discards; every newly kept plan plays out). Result on the full 450: 89.1% at ~7 search steps.

Finally: richer plan language

The remaining failures are mostly puzzles whose solutions cannot be said in the original subgoal vocabulary at all — a measured property of the language, not of training. Extending the language (tab 3) lifted the planner to 95.6%, two points under what the extended language provably permits.

the ceiling, measured

For some puzzles, no playable subgoal plan exists at all

An exhaustive no-network probe (try every plan the language can express, play each one out) settles, per puzzle, whether the planner's failure is the networks' fault or the language's.

Probing every one of the original planner's 49 base-benchmark failures: 22 puzzles cannot even be decomposed into subgoals, 20 decompose but no decomposition survives legal playback, and only 7 have a playable plan the networks missed. So 42 of 450 puzzles (9.3%) were structurally out of reach — no amount of training or extra search could help, which the cap-sensitivity re-probes confirm directly (quadrupling the search caps recovers none of them).

These are not unsolvable puzzles — the move-by-move planner solves 42/42 of them. They are puzzles whose solutions the subgoal vocabulary could not say: the only working plan needs a stopper standing on a wall-less cell, or a robot that steps aside and lets another pass, or one parked robot stopping two different sliders. one puzzle in full

A 4-move puzzle the original language could not express

Benchmark puzzle 333 (board env_2511), drawn from the real board file, with its optimal solution recomputed from the wall layout at build time. The move-by-move planner solves it; under the original plan language the subgoal planner provably never could.

The only way to solve it in 4 moves:

  1. Green slides up: (4, 5) → (4, 0)
  2. Yellow (the target) slides left: (6, 1) → (1, 1)
  3. Blue slides up: (4, 9) → (4, 1)
  4. Yellow (the target) slides right: (1, 1) → (3, 1)

Why the original language cannot say this. Look at cell (4, 1) (dashed outline). In move 2, Yellow slides straight through that cell — so it must be empty. In move 3, Blue parks exactly there — so that Yellow's final slide can bump into it and stop on the goal. The same cell has to be empty first and occupied later. And Blue can only stop at (4, 1) because Green is already parked at (4, 0) — a helper for the helper.

The original vocabulary could only say “park a helper on a cell — it stays put forever — and bounce off it.” It had no words for “let me pass first, then park behind me.” The exhaustive probe confirms this is fatal here: it tested 0 complete plans and provably ran out of options — the puzzle was not merely hard, it was unwritable.

Epilogue. Under the extended language this exact puzzle became expressible: the probe now finds a playable plan of 4 moves — exactly the optimum — and its plan diagram is the wall-less-stopper example drawn above.

why this matters for scale

The language ceiling widens with scale — and the extensions close it

The same exhaustive probe, run at 6 robots on all of the original planner's failures.

Of the original planner's 105 failures at 6 robots, 71 are structural (48 with no complete plan, 23 with no playable one) — the language-failure share of the whole 450-puzzle benchmark grows from 9.3% at base scale to 15.8% at 6 robots. Language failure grows exactly where the thesis needs the subgoal planner to win.

After the extensions, proven impossibility disappears: B1 leaves 2 puzzles proven impossible (of the original 71), and the B2 probe flips both of those too — 0 puzzles remain proven impossible at 6 robots. The conservative ceiling reads 84.2% → ≥96.9% → ≥98.0% (unresolved probes counted as failures). The scaling tab shows what the richer language does to the head-to-heads at every rung.

why scale is the real question

Exact search runs out first — that is what makes the thesis matter

If exact methods scaled, neither neural planner would be needed. They do not: the exact solver that labels training data and grades solutions fails on a growing share of puzzles as boards and robot counts grow.

training fragility

The move-by-move recipe needs hand-tuning at every new scale

The training recipe that works out of the box at the base scale destabilized at 6 robots, 8 robots, 24×24/8 and 32×32 — each time rescued by a lower learning rate. The tuning cost is itself scaling evidence, honestly caveated: every point has so far been rescuable. Per the integrity rule, a head-to-head row publishes only against a properly trained opponent.

what is matched today

The protocol's matched unit: one search step, same everywhere

Both planners run under a cap of 1,200 search expansions per puzzle with top-5 proposals per decision, on the identical pinned instances (checksummed per rung and set).

One expansion means the same thing in both families: pop the most promising node and generate its candidate continuations — one proposal-network pass plus one (batched) value-network pass. That makes the step budgets directly comparable at the network-call level: neither planner gets more neural computation per step than the other. Every head-to-head on this page is paired — the same puzzles, the same caps, the same scoring.

As recorded in every result file: “one popped search node whose children are generated (= 1 policy pass + 1 batched value pass over <= k children, in both systems)”

statistical rigour

Which differences survive a test

Every head-to-head on this page is paired — both planners face the same puzzles under the same budget — so each comparison is tested with an exact two-sided McNemar test on the discordant pairs (the puzzles exactly one side solved). The 95% confidence intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples boards rather than puzzles, because up to three puzzles share a board's wall layout and are not independent.

As recorded in the file: “exact two-sided McNemar on discordant pairs” · “percentile bootstrap, 10000 resamples of BOARDS (clustered; puzzles share a board's wall layout), seed 0” · “positional row match; refused unless both files record the same protocol.instances_sha256”

the honest ledger

What the expansion counter does not see

The matched unit counts network passes. The subgoal planner also does bookkeeping work that the counter does not meter — listed here in full, because a fair comparison must either count all work or measure the exclusion.

Physics checks inside the search

The checked search test-plays every partial plan as it is built (the prefix filter). These are pure board-physics slide simulations — no network calls — and they are not charged to the expansion budget.

“Free” forced-exact fixes

When a decision has exactly one viable candidate, the search commits it without spending an expansion. Cheap by construction, but uncounted.

Zero-cost pops of unplayable plans

A complete plan that fails its playback test is discarded and the search continues; the pop-and-discard is not charged as an expansion.

Deterministic park repairs

The full-language search may repair a failed complete plan by scheduling step-asides — a deterministic, network-free procedure, also outside the counter.

Why this matters: the hierarchical-search literature's standard (“What Matters in Hierarchical Search”, §4.3) is that a two-level method must either count all work at both levels or measure the excluded work and show it is negligible. The expansion counter here meters exactly the network computation — the dominant cost by design — but the four mechanisms above are real work done only on the subgoal side, so the honest position is to measure them rather than assert them away. Two complementary checks exist today: wall-clock time (below), which counts everything including the unmetered bookkeeping, and the instrumented counters now being collected.

The exclusion is not one-sided, and saying so was an error. The expansion counter meters network passes, so it misses the forward planner's physics too: generating one move-level successor set calls the board simulator once per (robot, direction). Measured in the unit both systems share — invocations of simulate.slide — a pilot over five identical base puzzles reads 64 slide calls for the subgoal planner against 5,776 for the move-level planner (medians, counting search physics only), so the unmetered work is larger on the side the ledger above does not list. That figure deliberately excludes a further ~34,700 move-level slides per puzzle spent featurizing states for its network: the raw totals are 983 against 40,448, but most of the move-level total is network input encoding rather than search, while the subgoal planner's own featurization reads precomputed tables and calls the primitive never — quoting the raw ratio would pass featurization off as physics. The qualification that survives is about the shape of the subgoal planner's cost rather than its size: its distribution is heavy-tailed, and a single unsolved puzzle in that pilot consumed more physics than the most expensive puzzle the move-level planner solved, almost all of it inside park repair. Accounting is therefore reported as median plus tail, never as a mean.

Instrumented counters have landed (eval/results/compute_accounting.json) — render support for this file's schema should be reviewed in the next report build pass.

the rules of the comparison

Protocol

Identical budgets

Every head-to-head runs at a cap of 1,200 search steps per puzzle with top-5 proposals per decision, on pinned per-configuration benchmarks of 450 puzzles (SHA-recorded instance files; the gradable/beyond-oracle split follows the exact solver's own success). One search step = examine one position or partial plan and generate its candidate continuations; in both families a step costs one pass of each network, so budgets are directly comparable.

Definition recorded in the result files: “one popped search node whose children are generated (= 1 policy pass + 1 batched value pass over <= k children, in both systems)”

Playable-moves scoring

A puzzle counts as solved only if the produced plan plays out legally, move by move, under full physics (eval/realize.py). “Extra moves” is the played-out length minus the exact optimum, averaged over solved puzzles; “% at optimum” is the share of solved puzzles at exactly the optimum. The subgoal planner's internal plan costs are never compared to move optima.

The oracle's role

The exact solver produces training labels and reference optima only — it never participates at solve time. Recorded with the base benchmark: “exact move-optimal cost returned by sample_instance (move_planner.oracle.solve); reference only, never used at inference”. On puzzles it cannot solve, result files carry placeholder optima — this report never renders solution-quality columns for those pools.

Machines and timing

Results before 2026-07-20 were measured on the origin machine; from 2026-07-20 on the Karolina cluster (CPU lanes of EPYC 7763 nodes). The tag is derived from each file's own recorded run date. Solve rates, search steps and move counts are machine-independent; wall-clock seconds are compared only within one machine, and tables that mix machines tag every seconds cell.

study design

Two structures × two ways to train

Four systems span the design space: the plan structure axis (move-by-move vs subgoals) crossed with the training axis (supervised on exact-solver labels vs self-play on the planner's own playable solutions). Supervised training is the quality path while the oracle lives; self-play is the only path that survives where the oracle fails. Both structures have working versions of both — the base-scale tab measures all four.

Data generation for training runs on a verified Rust port of the labeler (50–180× faster; 20,649 replayed decision contexts with zero label differences on pinned inputs — rust_datagen/VERIFICATION.md). Two disclosed findings from that verification: the original Python labeler's tie-breaking was order-dependent (≈7% of instances at the trajectory level, never within a trajectory), and a rare cost-estimate bug (≈0.02% of records) made some “exact” labels slightly off — negligible for training, recorded for honesty.

One asymmetry is meaningful, because it holds the boards fixed: at every scaling configuration both systems train on the same 700 boards, and the forward planner receives many times more supervision records from them — between 10.6× and 18.5× the backward planner's count (smallest at 16×16 board, 6 robots, largest at 32×32 board, 4 robots). Data volume is half of the training story; the other half — who got tuned, and how — is recorded in the two-sided tuning ledger at analysis/tuning_ledger.md. Three of its facts belong next to this table. First, the move-by-move planner needed a per-scale learning-rate rescue at four of the six configurations, each time after observed validation collapse; its stock recipe survived unchanged only at the base scale and at 24×24 with 4 robots. Second, the subgoal planner's value networks are warm-started from a banked predecessor checkpoint at every configuration — a stability mitigation with no forward counterpart — plus a lower learning rate at six or more robots and memory-driven batch caps. Third, neither side ever received a learning-rate sweep and both are single-seed everywhere, so no small difference between the systems is defensible against seed variation. The ledger also names the asymmetry that favours the move-by-move planner: its reported base-scale cell is the best of four independently trained systems, judged against a single subgoal run.

both planners attempt the same puzzles, so each puzzle gives a matched pair of outcomes; only the puzzles where they disagree carry information about which is better
