Ricochet Robots: two ways to plan, measured on the same puzzles

Ricochet Robots is a sliding-robot puzzle. A few robots sit on a walled grid; a moved robot slides in a straight line until it hits a wall or another robot — it cannot stop mid-slide. One robot is the target, one cell is the goal: get the target robot onto the goal cell in as few moves as possible.

This page compares two families of learned solvers on that puzzle. The forward planner plays the game the obvious way — one robot move at a time from the start toward the goal. The backward planner reasons from the goal outward, in larger units called subgoals — stepping-stones such as “a helper robot must first be parked here so the final slide stops on the goal” — each worth several moves at once. Everything on this page is generated from the experiments’ own result files; nothing is typed in by hand.

the game

How one move works

Robots slide; they cannot stop themselves. In the sketch, the red robot pushed right slides across the whole board and only stops at the border wall. The yellow robot — the target, marked with a halo — pushed down stops in the cell just above the blue robot, and that cell happens to be its goal (the dashed square).

That second slide is the heart of every puzzle: to stop somewhere in the open, a robot needs something to bump into. Good solutions therefore park helper robots at exact spots first, then bounce the target off them. The two planner families differ only in how they think about that: move by move, or bounce-spot by bounce-spot.

the contestants

Two planners, one recipe

Both families use the same machinery: a proposal network suggests what to try next, a value network estimates how close each option is to done, and a best-first search — one that always explores the most promising option first — ties the two together. They differ only in what one search decision commits to.

forward planner

Thinks exactly like the game: “which robot slides which way right now?” It builds the solution one real move at a time from the start position, so anything it finds is legal by construction — but it must search through many positions to find it.

backward planner

Thinks from the goal outward: “for the final slide to stop on the goal, a robot must first be parked HERE — and getting one there needs THIS first.” One such subgoal is worth several moves, so its searches are short — but its plans are written in shorthand and must survive being played out.

search step
the search examines one position (or partial plan) and generates its candidate continuations. In both families one step costs one pass of each network, so step budgets are directly comparable.
playable
a backward plan counts as solved only if it converts into an actual legal move sequence on the real board, with every robot present. A plan that only works on paper does not count.
extra moves
how many moves a found solution used beyond the shortest possible solution for that puzzle (0 = perfect).
the oracle
an exact, slow solver that knows true optimal answers. It is used only to create training labels and reference optima for scoring — never while the planners solve.

How the backward planner got from 53% to 96%

  1. Honest scoring first. Every plan was forced to actually play out, move by legal move, on the real board. On paper the planner had an answer for every single puzzle (450 of 450) — but only 53.1% of the plans really played out. That is the honest starting point.
  2. Four specific coding defects repaired — a robot could be scheduled to bounce off itself; one robot could hold two jobs at once; a plan could claim a stopper cell without ever placing a robot there; and plan steps were executed in a stricter order than intended. Each fix was accepted only after an A/B test showed zero regressions. → 80.0% on the matched 150-puzzle slice.
  3. Physics-checking moved inside the search. Instead of finishing a plan and only then discovering it cannot be played, every part-plan is checked as it is built and doomed branches are dropped immediately (verified: zero wrongly dropped). → 89.1%.
  4. First language extension. Two new things a plan may say: a stopper may stand on a wall-less cell (held there only by another robot or by timing), and a robot may briefly step aside to clear one specific slide. The networks were retrained on the new vocabulary. → 95.3% (and the provable ceiling moved 90.7% → 97.6%).
  5. Second language extension. A plan may now re-use an already-placed robot: the same parked robot can stop two different sliders, the main robot itself can serve as a bounce point mid-route, and a placed helper can move on to a second post. → 95.6% with the SAME networks, ceiling → 99.6%.

Each step is verified against stored results at build time. The per-stage table and the why-plans-fail tab carry the details, and the plan diagrams draw the structures.

On small puzzles the move-by-move planner wins on solution quality and the subgoal planner wins on effort — by one to two orders of magnitude. As puzzles grow, three measured things happen: the exact solver that move-by-move training depends on dies first; the subgoal planner keeps training itself where that solver is gone; and on the hardest puzzles beyond the solver's reach, the subgoal planner — once its plan language was made rich enough — now solves the most puzzles at a fraction of the work. Details in detailed results and scaling.

search effort

How much searching each system needs

forward (one move at a time)backward (over subgoals)

The two formulations spend their budget very differently on the same puzzles. The backward planner completes a plan after about 2.9 search steps on average, because one backward step commits an entire stepping-stone — several moves at once — and precomputed exact travel-distance tables absorb the movement between stepping-stones. The forward planners need 36 to 424 steps depending on how they were trained, because each step commits only a single move. That is the trade in one sentence: the backward planner compresses the search by one to two orders of magnitude, and pays for it with plans that are not always playable as written; the forward planners pay with far more searching and get plans that are legal by construction. Which cost matters more shifts with problem size — the scaling tab measures exactly that.

the playability story

From “found a plan” to “the plan actually plays”

The backward planner writes its plans in shorthand: “park a helper here, bounce the target off it, done.” Early measurements graded that shorthand — a plan counted as a solution if it was internally complete. Forced through the real rules of the game, move by legal move, many of those plans turned out to be impossible to play. This tab tells that story start to finish: the bugs that were fixed, the search change that finished the climb, the hard ceiling that remained — and how making the plan language richer then moved that ceiling.

Companion pages: see the plan structures drawn out — before and after each extension · browse the failure-example gallery (both open beside this report).

step 1 · the repairs

Most of the gap was bugs — four fixes, each verified

The backward planner almost always finds a plan in its own shorthand. The honest question is whether the plan survives contact with the real board. Four coding defects accounted for most of the early failures; each was fixed and re-measured on the same matched set (the first 150 benchmark puzzles), with no change to how the method works.

b keep-searching mode: whenever the search completes a plan, the plan is test-played first; if it fails, the search discards it and keeps looking instead of returning it.

fix 1 · no bouncing off yourself

Every backward step names a stopper — the robot the slider will bump into. A bug let the proposal network nominate the sliding robot as its own stopper. Fine on paper; impossible on a real board. Such proposals are now forbidden during search.

fix 2 · flexible order when converting to moves

Some plans only play out if the moving robot walks up close first and the helper parks second. The converter always parked the helper first. It now retries a failing step in the walk-up-first order — no other reordering, no extra moves added.

fix 3 · one robot, one job

A plan could recruit the same robot under two identities — say, as the stopper for two different slides in two places at once. Plans may no longer assign one robot two roles.

fix 4 · promised stoppers must be placed

A plan could claim “the slider stops here because a robot is parked there” without any step that actually parks one. A declared stopper must now really be placed by the plan.

Each fix was accepted only after an A/B test against the stored benchmark: for fix 2, all 239 previously working plans still produce exactly the same move counts (0 regressions), and 44 of the 211 previously failing plans now play out fully, verified move by move against the game physics (eval/results/realizer_twophase_ab.json).

step 2 · checking playability inside the search

Don’t finish doomed plans — drop them the moment they break

After the fixes, one structural improvement finished the climb. Instead of building a whole plan and only then test-playing it, the search now physics-checks every partial plan as it is built: the moment a plan’s already-committed steps cannot be played out, that branch of the search is discarded and the budget goes to branches that can still succeed.

Result on the full benchmark: 401/450 (89.1%) playable, 2.14 extra moves on average, 7.1 search steps and 1.07 s per puzzle (eval/results/final450_backward_prefix.json). The planner now refuses to build unplayable plans rather than discovering the problem afterwards.

Safety check: across the verification set, the filter discarded 0 branches it should have kept (zero false discards), and the solved count moved from 120 to 130 of 150 on the matched slice (eval/results/prefix_check_ab.json).

This same check matters beyond the benchmark score: it is what lets self-play train only on plans that really play out (self-play section).

How the ceiling was measured

Every one of the 49 puzzles the best backward mode fails was re-solved with the hand-written subgoal search — the neural networks removed entirely, every budget cap lifted — running exhaustively until it either found a plan that plays out or provably ran out of plans to try. Because the networks are out of the loop, “bad guidance” is ruled out as an explanation: whatever this search cannot do, no network-guided version of it can do either. Every puzzle resolved conclusively, each in seconds (analysis/artifacts/ceiling_probe.py → ceiling_probe_results.json). The 49 failures split three ways:

Plans exist, none survives physics20 Complete plans can be written, but every single one fails when played out move by move on the real board. No — every findable plan is a dud.
A playable plan exists — the networks missed it7 A working plan is out there; the network-guided search never generated it within its budget. Yes — this part is genuine headroom.

“Is that unfixable?” — the honest answer

not fixable by training or more search

For 42 of the 450 puzzles (9.3%) there is no playable plan to find: a planner that can only say “park a helper robot on this cell and bounce off it” has no correct thing to say about them, no matter how good its networks get or how long it searches. That puts a hard ceiling of 90.7% on this benchmark. The planner already scores 89.1%, so even perfect networks would add only about 1.6 points (7 puzzles). And they are poor recoveries: the plans that do exist need 6–24 moves against optima of 3–11; only 1 of the 7 is optimal.

fixable by extending the plan language

Today a helper parked as a stopper is assumed to stay put forever. The scoped extension adds new things a plan may say: a stopper robot may stand on a wall-less cell (held in place only by another robot or by timing), and a robot may briefly step aside to clear the way for one specific slide. That is exactly the maneuver the inexpressible puzzles need (see the worked example below). That extension has since been implemented and re-measured — the next section shows the ceiling moving.

Two things worth stating plainly. First, this ceiling is about expressiveness, not difficulty: among the inexpressible puzzles is one whose optimal solution is just 4 moves long, and the unplayable-plans group starts at 3 moves. Easy puzzles hit it too. Second, a clean negative would also be a real result: if extending the vocabulary cannot lift the ceiling economically, that is itself the strongest evidence that the move-by-move formulation is the better vehicle at this scale — which is exactly the question this project exists to answer.

Two additions were made to what a plan may say, both staying strictly inside the subgoal way of thinking (no raw moves were added to plans): a stopper robot may now stand on a wall-less cell — a cell it can only be held on because another robot, or the timing of the plan itself, stops it there — and a robot may be scheduled to slide aside just before a specific step of the plan runs, clearing the way. Every such maneuver is costed as ordinary moves; nothing is free. Recovery quality is good: 7 of the 38 newly expressible puzzles are solved move-optimally, with a median of 3 extra moves; 13 needed the step-aside maneuver.

The language is only half the job — the networks had never seen the new kinds of plan step, so they were retrained on examples written in the richer language. Result on the full benchmark: 429/450 (95.3%) solved playably at 9.7 search steps per puzzle — up from the pre-extension best in the table above, and about 2.2 points under the new ceiling (eval/results/final450_backward_b1.json).

The second extension: re-using robots the plan already placed — 97.6% → 99.6%

The first extension still could not express plans in which a robot does two jobs. The second one adds exactly that: the plan can now re-use a robot it already placed — the same parked robot can stop two different sliders, the main robot itself can serve as a bounce point mid-route, and a placed helper can slide on to a second post — and the step-aside repair can clear two robots at once, or step aside twice. As before, every extra maneuver is costed as ordinary moves.

Re-probing the 11 remaining puzzles with the fuller language: 9 now have a playable plan, and none is proven impossible. Counting the unresolved ones as failures — the conservative reading — the ceiling moves from 97.6% to 99.6% (448 of 450; analysis/artifacts/ceiling_probe_results_b2.json). The other 2 are unresolved, not proven impossible: their exhaustive probes ran out of search memory before either finding a plan or exhausting every option — an open search-budget question, not a proven wall. For the first time, no puzzle on this benchmark is proven unsolvable for the subgoal planner. 1 of the 9 recovered puzzles solved move-optimally; 1 needed two robots cleared out of the way at once — the exact case the first extension could not say.

The learned planner was re-run with the new machinery but the same trained networks (they have never seen the new kind of plan step): 430/450 (95.6%) solved playably at 9.7 search steps — only 1 more than before this extension. That gap is the honest reading: the language now permits about 99.6%, the networks achieve 95.6%, and closing the difference is a training problem — retraining on examples written in the full language is the scoped next step (eval/results/final450_backward_b2.json).

At 6 robots — the scale where the ceiling matters most — the same re-probe over the 14 previously unrecovered puzzles finds a playable plan for 5, with 9 still unresolved at their search-memory caps and 0 proven impossible. Counting every unresolved probe as a failure, the 6-robot ceiling is now at least 98.0% (441 of 450; scaling/results/g16r6/ceiling_probe_b2.json). Notably, both puzzles that were proven impossible under the first extension flipped to solvable (1 of them at exactly its move optimum) — so at 6 robots, nothing is proven impossible any more.

Safety check: all 249 stored plans from before the change convert to moves identically under the extended converter (249 of 249 equal, 0 mismatches) — the richer language adds words without changing the meaning of any existing plan (eval/results/realizer_b1_ab.json).

The same held for the second extension: 249 of 249 stored plans convert identically (eval/results/realizer_b2_ab.json).

And with the second extension switched off, re-running the original-language and the first-extension probes reproduces their checked-in results row-for-row — nothing about the default behavior changed (analysis/artifacts/ceiling_probe_*_recheck_post_b2.json).

step 5 · one puzzle, in full

A 4-move puzzle the plan language cannot express

Benchmark puzzle 333 (board env_2511), drawn from the real board file with its real optimal solution, recomputed from the wall layout at build time. The forward planner solves it; under the original plan language the backward planner never can — here is exactly why.

The only way to solve it in 4 moves:

  1. Green slides up: (4, 5) → (4, 0)
  2. Yellow (the target) slides left: (6, 1) → (1, 1)
  3. Blue slides up: (4, 9) → (4, 1)
  4. Yellow (the target) slides right: (1, 1) → (3, 1)

Why no subgoal plan can say this. Look at cell (4, 1) (dashed outline). In move 2, Yellow slides straight through that cell — so it must be empty. In move 3, Blue parks exactly there — so that Yellow’s final slide can bump into it and stop on the goal. The same cell has to be empty first and occupied later. And Blue can only stop at (4, 1) because Green is already parked at (4, 0) — a helper for the helper.

A subgoal can only say “park a helper on a cell — then it stays put — and bounce off it.” It has no words for “let me pass first, then park behind me.” The exhaustive no-network probe confirms this is fatal here: it built 0 complete plans before provably running out of options (22 search steps to exhaust every possibility) — the puzzle is not merely hard for the backward planner, it is unwritable in its language. The proposed “step aside / arrive late” stopper described above is precisely the missing word.

Epilogue. After the plan language was made richer (previous section), this exact puzzle became expressible: the probe now finds a playable plan of 4 moves — exactly the optimum (analysis/artifacts/ceiling_probe_results_b1.json).

the thesis

Do subgoals win as puzzles grow?

At the base scale the forward planner wins on quality and the backward planner on effort. The project’s driving hypothesis is that the balance tips backward as puzzles grow — more robots, bigger boards — for two reasons: the forward planner’s search cost grows with solution and travel length, and its supervised training depends on an exact solver that itself stops being computable. This tab collects the evidence, rung by rung.

why scale is the real question

The exact solver — the forward planner’s teacher — dies first

Supervised training for the forward planner needs an exact solver to label every training example, and the same solver provides the reference optima for scoring. As boards grow and robots multiply, that solver starts failing even with generous limits — so supervised training runs out of teacher, scoring loses its reference, and self-play becomes the only training that still works. Whichever family copes better with that is the family that scales.

Is that a bug or a wall? Measured directly: all 184 of the 8-robot puzzles the exact solver failed were re-run at ten times its normal search budget. 75/184 (40.8%) became solvable at that price; 109/184 (59.2%) stayed out of reach even then. The solver is complete in principle — this is a cost explosion, not a defect, and it moves outward with scale (scaling/data/g16r8/cap_probe_10x_results.jsonl).

Every completed rung, in full

The flip grows with robot count. On the 184 eight-robot puzzles too hard for the exact solver, the subgoal planner with the full plan language (networks unchanged) solves 163/184 (88.6%) vs the move-by-move planner's 93/184 (50.5%), at 4.5× fewer search steps and 9.5× less time. Big board and many robots at once. On the 289 hardest 24×24, 8-robot puzzles, even with the ORIGINAL plan language the subgoal planner solves 154/289 (53.3%) vs the move-by-move planner's 44/289 (15.2%), at 4.2× fewer search steps and 5.8× less time — the move-by-move planner spends nearly its whole search budget per puzzle and still rarely finishes. The frontier flip. On the 134 six-robot puzzles too hard for the exact solver, the subgoal planner (full plan language, networks unchanged) solves 108/134 (80.6%) vs the move-by-move planner’s 65/134 (48.5%), using 3.1× fewer search steps and 14.4× less time. the rules of the comparison

Protocol

  • Same puzzles. One pinned set of 450 puzzles (16×16 board, 4 robots) is used for every base-scale number on this page (eval/data/bench450.jsonl, sha256 1b384dfd375c161b…, generated 2026-07-09T16:52:26). Each scaling rung pins its own set the same way.
  • Same budget. Every system runs best-first search capped at 1200 search steps per puzzle, keeping the top 5 suggested continuations per step. In both families one step costs one pass of each network, so budgets are directly comparable.
  • Strict scoring. All costs are counted in real moves. A backward plan counts as solved only if it converts into a legal move sequence with every robot present (eval/realize.py) — plans that only work on paper do not count.
  • No exact solver at solve time. The planners are networks + search only. The exact solver is used solely to create training labels and reference optima; where it fails (large configurations), solving is scored by self-certification — a plan that plays out legally to the goal proves itself.
the study design

Two structures × two ways to train = four systems

supervised (copy the exact solver)self-play (no solver)
forward / move-by-move the “supervised” and “candidate-scored” headline rows the “warm start” and “from scratch” headline rows
backward / subgoals the backward headline rows the self-play section of the results tab

Both structures share the same neural-network encoder design (a transformer that passes messages along the board’s slide graph); they do not share weights. “Self-play” everywhere on this page means the planner trains on its own search’s solutions — there is no adversary and no Monte-Carlo tree search anywhere in the project.

fine print

Footnotes and provenance

  1. Search steps are not the same size in the two families. One backward search step commits an entire stepping-stone — a multi-move commitment (the moving robot's approach plus a helper placement) — while one forward step commits a single move. A shared cap therefore grants the backward planner strictly more solution-building work per step; the matched budget is generous to the backward system, which is the conservative direction for this comparison.
  2. The backward planner gets extra precomputed knowledge. Its environment includes exact shortest-travel-distance tables for each board, used during planning; the forward planner sees only the wall geometry. Part of the backward planner's step-count advantage comes from those tables, not from the networks.
  3. Two cost units exist and only one is honest. The backward planner's internal move estimate prices each plan step as if only its intended helper robot were on the board, so it can undercount real game moves. In the full 450-puzzle run, 65 of 450 plans claimed an internal move count below the known optimum — impossible as played, and direct proof the two units must not be mixed. Every comparable number on this page uses the strict count: moves of a plan that was actually executed legally, move by move.
  4. Seconds are machine-dependent; steps are not. Rows were produced in different eras on different machines (the study moved to a supercomputer partway through), so wall-clock seconds should only be compared between the two planners within the same row — never across rows. Search-step counts are machine-independent and are the right cross-row efficiency measure.
